The Daily Brief

Fire on the Mountain: How Haiti Shook the World

The revolution that the age of revolution tried hardest to forget.

---

The Hook

On the night of August 22, 1791, a fire appeared on the northern horizon of Saint-Domingue that would not go out for thirteen years. It began in the cane fields of the Plaine du Nord — a deliberate, organized act of arson that spread from plantation to plantation until the sky above Cap-Français glowed the color of a forge. Plantation owners fled toward the coast in their nightclothes. Overseers who had wielded the whip for years discovered, with sudden and terrible clarity, that the world they had built on the labor of enslaved people was as flammable as sugar cane. What followed was not merely a slave revolt, not merely a colonial uprising, but the most radical political transformation of the modern era: the only successful revolution in history led by enslaved people, culminating in the founding of the first Black republic in the Western Hemisphere. The United States had declared that all men were created equal and then preserved slavery. France had proclaimed liberty, equality, and fraternity and then tried to reimpose bondage when it proved economically inconvenient. Haiti did something neither of them could manage: it meant what it said.

---

The World Before

To understand what exploded in 1791, you have to understand what Saint-Domingue was — because it was, by almost any measure, the most valuable piece of real estate on the planet.

By the late eighteenth century, this French colony occupying the western third of the island of Hispaniola produced roughly forty percent of Europe's sugar and more than half of its coffee. It was the engine of the French Atlantic economy, a place where fortunes were made with staggering speed and human beings were consumed at an equally staggering rate. The mortality rate among enslaved people in Saint-Domingue was so brutal that the population required constant replenishment from the African slave trade — an estimated thirty thousand enslaved Africans arrived each year just to maintain the labor force. Historians estimate that by 1789, approximately five hundred thousand enslaved people lived in Saint-Domingue, outnumbering the white colonists by roughly ten to one.

The social architecture of the colony was a pyramid of carefully maintained cruelties. At the top sat the grand blancs — wealthy white planters who dreamed of independence from French mercantile regulation, not unlike the American colonists who had recently won their own revolution. Below them were the petit blancs, poor and middling whites who compensated for their economic precarity with ferocious racial pride. Then came the gens de couleur libres — free people of color, many of them mixed-race and some of them extraordinarily wealthy, who owned land and enslaved people themselves but were barred by law from sitting with white men in public spaces, from wearing European clothing in certain contexts, from walking beside a white person on the street. And at the base, holding up the entire edifice with their bodies, were the enslaved.

The French Revolution, which began in 1789, sent shockwaves through this volatile arrangement. When the Declaration of the Rights of Man arrived in Saint-Domingue, everyone immediately asked the same question in their own interest: does this apply to us? The grand blancs wanted political autonomy. The free people of color, led by men like Vincent Ogé, demanded civil equality with whites. The enslaved said nothing publicly — but they were listening, organizing, and waiting.

---

The Revolution as Story

The night of August 22 was not spontaneous. It had been planned at a ceremony in the forest of Bois Caïman just days before, where an enslaved man named Boukman Dutty, a Vodou houngan and coachman, presided over a gathering that blended political conspiracy with religious ritual. A pig was sacrificed. Oaths were sworn. The precise nature of what happened at Bois Caïman has been contested ever since — the ceremony was reconstructed largely from hostile and secondhand sources — but its symbolic weight in Haitian memory is immense. It marks the moment when the enslaved of Saint-Domingue ceased to be objects of history and became its subjects.

Boukman died in battle within weeks, but the uprising he helped ignite could not be killed. The colonial government offered amnesties, then rescinded them. Spain and Britain, sensing an opportunity to pry this jewel from France's crown, sent armies. The British occupied much of the colony from 1793 to 1798, losing somewhere between forty thousand and sixty thousand soldiers to yellow fever and Haitian arms — a catastrophe that quietly shaped British strategic thinking for a generation.

Into this maelstrom stepped Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved man who had been granted his freedom some years before the revolution and who possessed one of the most formidable military and political minds of his age. Toussaint was not present at Bois Caïman. He joined the rebellion later, initially fighting under Spanish command before making a dramatic switch to the French side in 1794, shortly after France abolished slavery in its colonies — a decision driven in no small part by the revolution itself. He rose to become governor-for-life of Saint-Domingue, a man who corresponded with Napoleon Bonaparte as something approaching an equal, who wrote a constitution in 1801 that declared freedom for all and made himself head of state for life while nominally maintaining the fiction of French sovereignty.

Napoleon would not tolerate it. In 1801 he dispatched his brother-in-law Charles Leclerc with an army of perhaps forty thousand soldiers — the finest in Europe — to reassert French control and, it is now clear from the documents, to restore slavery. Toussaint was lured into negotiations and arrested in a betrayal that became legendary; he died in a cold French prison in April 1803, reportedly telling his captors: "In overthrowing me, you have done no more than cut down the trunk of the tree of Black liberty in Saint-Domingue. It will spring back from the roots, for they are numerous and deep."

He was right. Jean-Jacques Dessalines, Henri Christophe, and Alexandre Pétion took up the struggle. Yellow fever again devastated the French army. The final campaigns of 1803 were brutal on both sides — and the French committed atrocities of extraordinary savagery, including drowning prisoners and using dogs to kill captives, acts that were documented and that hardened Haitian resistance to steel. On November 18, 1803, the Battle of Vertières effectively ended French military power in the colony. On January 1, 1804, Dessalines declared independence, tearing the white from the French tricolor and sewing the blue and red together, and named the new nation Hayti — an indigenous Taíno word for the mountainous land.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Historians have argued for two centuries about nearly every dimension of this story, and the debates are not merely academic.

One long-running controversy concerns Toussaint himself: was he a genuine revolutionary, or a conservative figure whose primary goal was the restoration of plantation agriculture under a different management? His 1801 constitution maintained the plantation system and compelled formerly enslaved people to continue working the land — under conditions that were technically free but structurally coercive. C.L.R. James, in his magnificent 1938 study The Black Jacobins, portrayed Toussaint as a tragic hero undone by his own ambivalence, caught between the Black masses and the European world he admired. More recent scholars like Laurent Dubois have pushed back against overly tragic framings, emphasizing the genuine complexity of governing a society emerging from slavery while fighting multiple imperial powers simultaneously.

A second debate concerns the revolution's relationship to the broader Age of Revolution. For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the Haitian Revolution was systematically excluded from the canon of "great" modern revolutions — the American, French, and later Latin American independence movements. Scholars like Michel-Rolph Trouillot, in his essential Silencing the Past, argued that this exclusion was not accidental but ideological: the Haitian Revolution was "unthinkable" to contemporaries and later historians alike because it challenged the very foundations of racial hierarchy that made the Atlantic world function. The silence, Trouillot argued, was itself historical evidence.

A third, more recent area of inquiry concerns the role of Vodou and African religious traditions in sustaining the revolution. Earlier historians, working within colonial frameworks, either dismissed the religious dimensions entirely or treated them as primitive backdrop to the "real" political story. Scholars like Leslie G. Desmangles and more recently Kate Ramsey have argued that Vodou was not incidental to the revolution but constitutive of it — a network of belief, community, and communication that bound together people who had been deliberately separated by language, origin, and colonial design.

---

The Aftermath

Haiti's independence was purchased at an almost incomprehensible cost — and the world made sure of it.

France refused to recognize Haitian sovereignty until 1825, and then only in exchange for an indemnity of 150 million francs (later reduced to 90 million), ostensibly to compensate French slaveholders for their "lost property." Haiti paid this debt, with interest to French and American banks, until 1947. A 2022 investigation by The New York Times calculated the total cost, including compound interest and economic opportunity lost, at somewhere between 21 and 115 billion dollars. Haiti essentially spent the first century and a half of its existence paying reparations to the people who had enslaved its citizens.

The United States, whose slaveholding Southern states watched the Haitian Revolution with undisguised terror, refused to recognize Haiti until 1862 — when the Confederate states had left Congress and could no longer block it. Thomas Jefferson, who had spoken warmly with Toussaint's representatives, imposed a trade embargo. The fear was explicit: American slaveholders did not want their enslaved people to hear that liberation was possible, that it had been done, that it had worked.

Internally, Haiti struggled with the contradictions baked into its founding. Dessalines, who declared independence, was assassinated in 1806. The country split briefly into a northern kingdom under Christophe and a southern republic under Pétion. The plantation economy, which all factions attempted in various forms to maintain, ultimately collapsed — not as a failure but, one could argue, as a success: formerly enslaved people simply refused to work the cane fields on any terms that resembled what had come before. They became smallholders, subsistence farmers, people who chose poverty over the intimacy of plantation discipline. The economic consequences were severe and long-lasting. The political consequences — coups, instability, foreign intervention — were equally severe, and cannot be understood apart from the external pressures that never relented.

The revolution's influence radiated outward even as Haiti itself was being strangled. Simón Bolívar twice received Haitian sanctuary and material support from Pétion in his campaigns to liberate South America — support given on the condition that he free the enslaved in the territories he liberated. The revolution haunted every slaveholder in the Americas. It shaped the arguments of abolitionists and the nightmares of planters in equal measure.

---

Why It Matters

The Haitian Revolution sits at the intersection of every large question the modern world has been arguing about since the eighteenth century: the universality of human freedom, the relationship between race and capitalism, the right of the oppressed to use violence in pursuit of liberation, and the willingness of powerful nations to tolerate a genuine challenge to their economic and racial order.

It matters because it was real. Not a promise, not a theory, not a declaration signed by men who owned other men — but an actual transformation, accomplished at enormous cost by people who had everything stacked against them and who won anyway. It exposes, with uncomfortable clarity, the gap between the rhetoric of the Age of Revolution and its practice. The United States and France proclaimed universal liberty while maintaining slavery; Haiti abolished it in blood and fire and was punished for a century and a half for the audacity.

It matters, too, because the silence around it is itself instructive. The way the Haitian Revolution was written out of Western historical consciousness — or written in only as a cautionary tale of chaos and excess — tells us something important about whose stories are considered universal and whose are considered particular. The recovery of this history by scholars like Trouillot, James, and Dubois is not merely an act of academic justice; it is a recalibration of what modernity actually was and who actually made it.

And it matters because the questions it raised — about debt, sovereignty, the long economic shadow of slavery, the conditions under which genuine equality becomes possible — are not settled. They are the questions of our own moment, dressed in different clothes, still waiting for answers as serious as the ones the people of Saint-Domingue gave them on that burning August night in 1791.
