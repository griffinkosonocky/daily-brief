The Daily Brief

When the Church Put Itself on Trial

Wednesday — Medieval History

---

A City on the Edge of a Cliff

Imagine arriving in Constance in the autumn of 1414. You have ridden for weeks, perhaps from Paris or Prague or Cracow, your horse's hooves clicking over the stone bridge that spans the Rhine where it pours cold and green out of Lake Constance. The town is already bursting. Inns that normally house a dozen travelers are stacking men three to a bed. Merchants have erected canvas stalls along every lane. The smell of roasting meat mingles with the sharper tang of horse dung and lake water. Somewhere above it all, the unfinished spire of the cathedral reaches into an October sky like an argument that has not yet found its conclusion.

You are not alone. Over the next few months, somewhere between fifty thousand and one hundred thousand people will pour into a city whose normal population hovers around six thousand. Cardinals in scarlet robes jostle past wool merchants. Theologians from Oxford argue in Latin with scholars from Bologna. Knights in travel-stained surcoats guard the luggage of princes. Pickpockets, prostitutes, booksellers, and relics-hawkers fill every corner. One contemporary chronicler, the Ulrich von Richental, recorded the arrivals with the breathless precision of a man who cannot quite believe what he is witnessing.

The occasion was the Council of Constance — the largest gathering of Christian authority in the medieval world, convened to solve a problem so embarrassing, so structurally catastrophic, that the Church could no longer pretend it did not exist. There were, at that moment, three men who each claimed to be the one true Pope.

---

The World Before: A Church Divided Against Itself

To understand how Christianity's supreme institution arrived at this grotesque impasse, you have to go back a generation. In 1309, the papacy had relocated from Rome to Avignon, in southern France, under circumstances that were partly political and partly practical but were widely perceived — especially by Italians — as a French kidnapping of the Holy Father. For nearly seventy years, a succession of French popes ruled from a magnificent palace on the banks of the Rhône. Critics called it the "Babylonian Captivity of the Church," borrowing Old Testament language of exile and humiliation.

When Pope Gregory XI finally returned the papacy to Rome in 1377, he died shortly afterward, and the conclave that followed — held in Rome, under pressure from Roman mobs who wanted an Italian pope — elected Urban VI. Urban proved to be erratic and possibly abusive toward the cardinals, so a faction of them declared his election invalid and chose a rival, Clement VII, who returned to Avignon. Christendom now had two popes, each excommunicating the other. France, Scotland, and Castile backed Clement; England, the Holy Roman Empire, and most of Italy backed Urban. Every diocese in Europe had to choose sides, and every choice was also a political statement.

This "Great Schism" ground on for decades. When the cardinals of both obediences grew desperate enough to call their own council at Pisa in 1409 to resolve the crisis, they elected a third pope — Alexander V — without successfully deposing either of the existing claimants. The cure had tripled the disease. Three popes. Three curias. Three sets of appointments to every major church office. Three streams of revenue being extracted from the same parishes. The faithful were not merely confused; they were being bled.

Against this backdrop, a reform movement had been building for a century. John Wycliffe in England and Jan Hus in Bohemia had been arguing that the Church's institutional corruption — the selling of indulgences, the accumulation of wealth by bishops who never visited their dioceses, the political maneuvering that had produced the Schism itself — was not an accident but a symptom. The institution needed to be reformed at its root. Their ideas were dangerous precisely because they were not entirely wrong.

---

The Council Itself: Drama in Three Acts

The Council of Constance, which ran from November 1414 to April 1418, can be understood as a drama in three acts, each with its own protagonist and its own kind of violence — one intellectual, one judicial, one political.

The First Act: Ending the Schism

The council's most urgent task was to reunify the papacy. The sitting Pope, John XXIII (not to be confused with the twentieth-century pope who took the same name), had convened the council under pressure from the Holy Roman Emperor Sigismund, a forceful and politically shrewd ruler who understood that a fractured Church was a fractured empire. John XXIII hoped to control the proceedings. He did not.

When the council fathers voted to organize themselves by nations rather than by individual headcount — a procedural revolution that gave smaller delegations equal weight with the Italian bloc that dominated any headcount — John saw the writing on the wall. In March 1415, he fled Constance in disguise, reportedly dressed as a groom, hoping that his absence would dissolve the council. The council fathers, invoking the doctrine of conciliarism — the idea that a general council held authority superior to any individual pope — declared that they could continue regardless. They then put John XXIII on trial.

The charges read against him were startling in their specificity: simony, perjury, sodomy, murder by poison. Not all of these were proven to any modern evidentiary standard, and some may have been exaggerated by his enemies. But John was deposed. The Roman claimant, Gregory XII, was persuaded to resign with dignity — and in a face-saving gesture, he was allowed to formally "reconvoke" the council so that his line of succession could be said to have authorized it. The Avignon claimant, Benedict XIII, refused to budge and was simply declared deposed in absentia. He retreated to a castle on the Spanish coast and died still insisting he was the legitimate pope, a figure of tragicomic stubbornness.

In November 1417, the council elected a new pope: Oddo Colonna, who took the name Martin V. The Schism was over. One pope, one Church. The crowd in Constance reportedly wept.

The Second Act: The Trial of Jan Hus

While the cardinals maneuvered, a Bohemian priest named Jan Hus had arrived in Constance under a safe-conduct guarantee from Emperor Sigismund. Hus was a reformer in the tradition of Wycliffe — he had preached against indulgences, challenged the authority of corrupt clergy, and argued that Scripture should take precedence over papal decree. He was beloved in Bohemia and regarded as a heretic in Rome.

Hus believed he was coming to Constance to debate. He got a trial instead. The safe-conduct, Sigismund's councilors argued, did not protect a heretic from judgment by a general council. This was, even by medieval standards, a legalistic betrayal. Hus was imprisoned in a Dominican monastery, tried before the council, and given the opportunity to recant. He refused to recant positions he had never held, and he refused to abandon positions he believed were scripturally grounded. On July 6, 1415, he was taken to a meadow outside the city walls, stripped of his clerical vestments in an elaborate ceremony of degradation, crowned with a paper mitre painted with images of devils, and burned alive.

His last words, by various accounts, were a prayer. The executioners, following protocol, ground his bones and threw the ashes into the Rhine so that no relics could be preserved. They underestimated the power of martyrdom. Within months, Bohemia was in revolt. The Hussite Wars would convulse Central Europe for decades.

The Third Act: Reform That Wasn't

The third agenda item was the one most reformers had come to Constance hoping to advance: a structural reformation of the Church. The decree Frequens, passed in 1417, mandated that general councils meet regularly — every ten years — effectively institutionalizing conciliarism as a check on papal power. It was a constitutional moment, a genuine attempt to make the Church governable by something other than the will of one man.

It did not last. Once Martin V was elected and the emergency had passed, the papacy's interest in regular councils evaporated. Martin and his successors worked systematically to reassert papal supremacy, and the momentum of Frequens dissipated. The Council of Basel, which met in the 1430s, tried to revive conciliarism and ended in schism again. By the time the papacy had consolidated its Renaissance authority, the conciliarist moment had been outmaneuvered, though never quite forgotten.

---

The Scholarly Debate: Who Really Won?

Historians have argued for generations about what the Council of Constance actually accomplished and what it failed to do — and why.

The burning of Hus has attracted particular scrutiny. Was it inevitable, or did political calculation by Sigismund — who needed Bohemian support but also could not be seen protecting a heretic — seal Hus's fate? Some scholars argue that Hus himself bore some responsibility for his own inflexibility; others see in his refusal to recant a principled stand that changed European history. The Czech national tradition has long treated him as a founding martyr, a status that shaped how Bohemia understood itself for centuries.

The question of conciliarism is equally contested. Brian Tierney and his intellectual descendants argued that conciliarism represented a genuine constitutional tradition within the Church — a medieval form of representative government — that was suppressed by the papacy but never truly extinguished. Others, following the more papalist historiography, treat it as an emergency measure that was never intended to be permanent. The debate matters because it touches on whether the medieval Church contained within itself the seeds of its own reform, or whether those seeds required the violence of the sixteenth-century Reformation to finally germinate.

There is also the uncomfortable question of what "reform" meant in 1414. The council fathers wanted to end the Schism and curb the worst abuses of papal finance. Almost none of them wanted to question the theological foundations of the institution. The reformers who did — Hus, and behind him Wycliffe — were silenced by fire. The gap between institutional reform and doctrinal reform was one the council could not bridge, and that gap would eventually become the Reformation itself.

---

The Aftermath: Seeds in the Ash

Martin V returned to Rome and set about rebuilding a papacy that had been humiliated and fractured for nearly a century. He was competent and politically skilled, and under him and his successors the papacy began its transformation into the Renaissance institution — wealthy, artistically ambitious, politically engaged — that would both dazzle and scandalize Europe.

In Bohemia, the execution of Hus ignited the Hussite Wars. The Hussites, fighting under the banner of a chalice (they demanded that the laity receive communion wine, which the Church reserved for priests), proved unexpectedly formidable. Their general, Jan Žižka, developed mobile wagon-fort tactics that defeated crusade after crusade sent against them. Bohemia remained effectively outside Roman obedience for much of the fifteenth century — a preview of what confessional fracture could look like.

The conciliarist idea never entirely died. It resurfaced at the Council of Basel, influenced the Gallican tradition in France, and echoed through the debates of the Second Vatican Council in the twentieth century about the relationship between the papacy and the college of bishops. The question of how authority should be distributed within a large institution — by hierarchy or by representation — is not a question the fifteenth century invented, but Constance gave it one of its most dramatic rehearsals.

And the city itself? Constance returned to its normal size and its normal concerns. The Rhine still flows green beneath its bridges. The cathedral still stands, its spire eventually completed in the nineteenth century. A plaque near the lakeshore marks the approximate spot where Hus was burned.

---

Why It Matters

The Council of Constance sits at a crossroads that every large institution eventually reaches: the moment when the gap between its ideals and its practices becomes impossible to ignore, and when the people inside it must choose between genuine reform and the preservation of existing power.

The council chose, in the end, to preserve power — to end the embarrassment of three popes, to silence the most dangerous reformers, to pass constitutional decrees it had no real intention of enforcing. It was not a cynical institution; many of the people in that crowded, extraordinary city were sincere in their desire for a better Church. But sincerity is not the same as structural change, and the difference between them has consequences that compound over time.

A century after Constance, Martin Luther nailed his theses to the door at Wittenberg. The questions he was asking — about indulgences, about papal authority, about the relationship between Scripture and tradition — were recognizably the questions that Jan Hus had brought to Constance and carried to the fire. The Church had a century to answer them differently. It did not.

This is not a story about medieval irrelevance. It is a story about what happens when institutions mistake the resolution of a crisis for the resolution of the problem that caused the crisis. The Schism ended at Constance. The conditions that produced the Schism — unchecked authority, financial corruption, the suppression of legitimate criticism — did not. The reckoning was merely deferred, and deferred reckonings have a way of arriving with interest.
