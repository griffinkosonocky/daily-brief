The Daily Brief

The City at the Center of the World: Tang Chang'an

Medieval East Asia | Tang Dynasty, 618–907 CE

---

Imagine arriving at a city so vast that its outer walls alone stretch nearly nine kilometers from east to west. You have traveled for months — perhaps from Samarkand, perhaps from the Korean peninsula, perhaps from the jungles of northern Vietnam — and nothing in your experience has prepared you for what rises on the horizon. A grid of boulevards wider than any road you have ever walked. Towers and pagodas catching the morning light. The smell of incense and roasting meat and horse dung and foreign spice all braided together in the dry air of the Wei River plain. You are not merely entering a city. You are entering an argument about what civilization could become — and for a moment, in the seventh and eighth centuries of the common era, that argument was being won at Chang'an.

---

The World Before

To understand why Chang'an dazzled the medieval imagination, you have to understand how fragmented and exhausted the world around it had been.

China had spent nearly four centuries in pieces. After the Han dynasty collapsed in 220 CE, the empire fractured into competing kingdoms, was briefly reunited, and then shattered again under waves of migration and invasion from the steppes. The period historians call the Northern and Southern Dynasties was an era of relentless political violence, mass displacement, and — paradoxically — extraordinary cultural mixing. Buddhism arrived from India and transformed Chinese intellectual life. Nomadic peoples from the north settled in the Yellow River basin and gradually blended their customs with those of the sedentary populations they governed. New hybrid forms of art, music, and governance emerged from the wreckage.

The Sui dynasty (581–618 CE) reunited the empire with breathtaking speed and equally breathtaking brutality. Emperor Yang of Sui launched canal projects and military campaigns on a scale that bled the population white. His Grand Canal, linking the Yellow River to the Yangtze, was a genuine engineering marvel — and it killed hundreds of thousands in its construction. When Yang was assassinated by his own officers in 618, a general named Li Yuan stepped into the vacuum and declared a new dynasty. He called it the Tang.

The capital he chose was not a new city. Chang'an — the "Perpetual Peace" — had served as the seat of the Han emperors centuries earlier. But Li Yuan and his son, the brilliant and ruthless Emperor Taizong, rebuilt it on a scale that dwarfed anything that had come before. They were not merely building a city. They were building a statement.

---

The Story of the City

The Chang'an of the Tang dynasty was, by any reasonable measure, the largest city on earth for much of the seventh and eighth centuries. Its population at peak — scholars estimate somewhere between 800,000 and one million people within the walls, with additional hundreds of thousands in surrounding suburbs — made it a metropolis without parallel in the medieval world. Constantinople was impressive; Ctesiphon had been magnificent; Baghdad was rising. But Chang'an was something else entirely.

The city was laid out on a rigid grid, a physical expression of Confucian cosmological order. The imperial palace complex, the Daming Palace, sat at the northern end like the Pole Star around which everything else rotated — a deliberate echo of the Chinese understanding of heaven's arrangement. From there, the great central boulevard, Zhuque Avenue, drove south through the city like a spine, flanked by ward after ward of residential and commercial life. The city was divided into 108 rectangular wards, each enclosed by its own walls and gates that were locked at night by curfew bells. To move through Tang Chang'an was to move through a city that was simultaneously open and controlled, cosmopolitan and ceremonially ordered.

What made it genuinely extraordinary, though, was not the planning. It was the people.

The Tang emperors — particularly Taizong and his successors through the reign of the magnificent and controversial Empress Wu Zetian and into the golden reign of Emperor Xuanzong in the early eighth century — pursued a policy of deliberate openness that was unusual in the ancient world and almost unimaginable by later Chinese standards. Foreign merchants, diplomats, monks, scholars, and soldiers were not merely tolerated; they were actively recruited. The western market of Chang'an was a sensory riot of Central Asian goods and people: Sogdian merchants from the oases of modern Uzbekistan ran the silk trade with astonishing sophistication, operating credit networks that stretched from the Mediterranean to the Pacific. Persian Zoroastrian fire temples stood a short walk from Nestorian Christian churches. Manichaean communities worshipped alongside Buddhist monasteries of half a dozen different schools. There were Japanese students copying Tang administrative law to bring home to their islands. There were Tibetan ambassadors, Korean monks, Arab traders, and Indian physicians.

The Silk Road in this period was not a single road but a braided network of routes, and Chang'an was its eastern terminus — the place where the goods and ideas and faiths that moved along those routes accumulated and transformed. Tang poetry, the dynasty's most celebrated cultural achievement, is saturated with images of this world: the Central Asian lute called the pipa, the "grape wine in a luminous jade cup," the dancer from Sogdiana spinning in a whirl of crimson sleeves. When the poet Wang翰 wrote that a man should drink before battle because "how many soldiers have ever returned from war?" he was describing a world where wine came from the west and the wars were fought against Turkic confederations on the steppe. The cosmopolitan and the martial were inseparable.

The reign of Emperor Xuanzong (712–756 CE) represents something like Tang civilization's high watermark. The arts flourished. The great poets Li Bai and Du Fu were both alive, though they moved in very different registers — Li Bai the Daoist romantic, Du Fu the Confucian moralist. The economy was productive. The examination system, which allowed men of non-aristocratic birth to enter government service by passing rigorous written tests, was functioning as a genuine if imperfect meritocracy. Buddhism had been absorbed so thoroughly into Chinese culture that it had effectively become Chinese, generating homegrown schools like Chan (known later in Japan as Zen) that bore only a family resemblance to their Indian ancestors.

And then, in 755, everything broke.

A general named An Lushan — half Sogdian, half Turkic, a favorite of Xuanzong's court and the commander of three northeastern frontier armies — launched a rebellion that would nearly destroy the dynasty and certainly ended the world that Chang'an had represented. The An Lushan Rebellion lasted eight years and killed, by some scholarly estimates, tens of millions of people through direct violence, famine, and displacement. Xuanzong fled the capital in the night, and along the road his imperial guards mutinied and demanded the execution of Yang Guifei, the emperor's beloved consort whose family had accumulated enough power to become a symbol of everything wrong with the late Tang court. Xuanzong assented. Yang Guifei was strangled by a eunuch with a silk cord. The emperor wept and kept walking.

Chang'an was occupied by rebel forces. It was sacked. It was recovered. It was sacked again. When the Tang finally suppressed the rebellion in 763 — with significant help from Uyghur mercenaries, who extracted a humiliating price — they ruled over a dynasty that was a ghost of its former self. The great cosmopolitan experiment was over. The Silk Road networks fractured. The Sogdian merchants disappeared from history. The examination system contracted. The open policy toward foreigners curdled into suspicion. A massive persecution of Buddhism in 845, under Emperor Wuzong, destroyed thousands of monasteries and forced hundreds of thousands of monks and nuns back into lay life — a convulsion that permanently diminished Buddhism's institutional presence in China even as it was flourishing everywhere else in Asia.

The dynasty stumbled on until 907, when the last Tang emperor was deposed by a warlord and China once again dissolved into competing states.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Historians have long argued about what, exactly, Chang'an at its height represents. Was Tang China genuinely cosmopolitan in the modern sense — a society that valued diversity as a positive good — or was its openness more instrumental, a pragmatic tool of imperial power that welcomed foreigners when they were useful and discarded them when they were not?

The evidence points in uncomfortable directions. The Tang code of law treated foreigners with a degree of legal sophistication unusual for the era, but it also enforced strict rules about where foreigners could live and whom they could marry. The emperors patronized Buddhist institutions lavishly while also using them as political tools. The examination system opened opportunity to talent but was also structured in ways that favored those who could afford years of classical study — which meant, in practice, families of means.

There is also a deeper debate about continuity. Some scholars, following the work of historians like Mark Lewis, emphasize how much the Tang inherited from the hybrid North Asian cultures of the preceding centuries — arguing that the Tang "Chinese" identity was itself a construction, built on a foundation of Xianbei, Turkic, and Sogdian elements that the dynasty's founders worked hard to retrospectively obscure. The Li family that founded the Tang almost certainly had significant non-Han ancestry, and the culture they created was not the recovery of some pure Chinese essence but something genuinely new, forged in the crucible of the migration period.

This debate matters because it shapes how we understand the Tang's legacy. If Chang'an's openness was a feature of a specifically hybrid, post-migration Chinese culture, then the question becomes: what was lost when that culture closed off? And why did it close off so decisively?

---

The Aftermath

The Five Dynasties period that followed the Tang (907–960 CE) was brief but brutal. Five successive regimes rose and fell in the north while ten independent states fragmented the south. When the Song dynasty finally reunified most of China in 960, it did so in a world that had changed irreversibly.

The Song was, in many ways, a more sophisticated civilization than the Tang — more commercially developed, more technologically innovative, the birthplace of printing, the compass, and gunpowder weapons. But it was also more inward-looking, more anxious about its northern borders (which it never fully controlled), and more explicitly Han Chinese in its cultural self-understanding. The great cosmopolitan mixing of the Tang was not reproduced.

The legacy of Chang'an flowed outward rather than forward. Japan's ancient capitals of Nara and Kyoto were both modeled directly on Tang Chang'an, their grid plans a deliberate homage to the city that Japanese envoys had visited and marveled at. Korean kingdoms absorbed Tang administrative and legal structures wholesale. Vietnamese elites took Tang poetry as their literary standard. The examination system, in various adapted forms, spread across East Asia and endured in China until 1905. Buddhism, shaped by its Tang encounter with Chinese culture, radiated outward to become the dominant faith of Tibet, Mongolia, Korea, Japan, and much of Southeast Asia in forms that bore the unmistakable stamp of its Chinese transformation.

---

Why It Matters

Chang'an at its height was an experiment in what happens when a powerful state deliberately opens itself to the world — when it decides that the movement of people, goods, faiths, and ideas across its borders is a source of strength rather than a threat. The experiment produced extraordinary art, literature, and innovation. It also produced instability, resentment, and, eventually, a violent retrenchment.

The tension at the heart of Tang Chang'an is not an ancient curiosity. It is the permanent condition of any society wealthy and powerful enough to attract the world's attention. The question of how to remain open without becoming ungovernable, how to absorb difference without losing coherence, how to build institutions robust enough to survive the inevitable shocks — these are not questions that the Tang answered. They are questions that the Tang lived, and died by, in ways that still echo.

Every city that has ever called itself a crossroads, every empire that has ever claimed to welcome the stranger, every civilization that has looked at its own complexity and wondered whether it was a sign of health or a harbinger of dissolution — all of them are, in some sense, still walking the wide boulevards of Chang'an, trying to find their way to the palace at the northern end before the curfew bells ring and the gates close for the night.
