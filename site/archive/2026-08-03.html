The Daily Brief

How Europe Carved a Continent: The Scramble for Africa and the Berlin Conference

---

A Map Drawn in Smoke

Imagine a room in Berlin in the winter of 1884. Outside, the city is cold and grey, gas lamps flickering along the Wilhelmstrasse. Inside, fourteen nations — European powers and the United States — have gathered around a long table covered in maps. The maps are, in places, almost comically blank. Vast stretches of the African interior are marked with nothing more than rivers half-guessed at, mountain ranges approximated, and the honest admission of cartographic ignorance that Europeans had grown accustomed to calling "terra incognita." Within a generation, those blank spaces would be filled in — not by the people who lived there, but by the men in this room, drawing lines with rulers and fountain pens, dividing a continent they had never walked across among nations that had no claim to it.

It was one of the most consequential acts of collective hubris in modern history. And it happened, as so many world-altering things do, with a great deal of diplomatic courtesy, fine wine, and almost no acknowledgment of the fifty million or so people whose lives were being decided without their consent.

---

The World Before

To understand what happened in Berlin, you have to understand what Africa looked like — to Africans and to Europeans — before the scramble began in earnest in the early 1880s.

Africa in 1880 was not a blank slate. It was a continent of extraordinary political complexity. The Zulu Kingdom in southern Africa had, less than two years earlier, handed the British Army one of its most humiliating defeats at Isandlwana. The Sokoto Caliphate in West Africa was among the largest states in the world by population, governing millions under a sophisticated legal and administrative structure. The Kingdom of Buganda on the shores of Lake Victoria was a highly centralized monarchy with its own bureaucracy, army, and diplomatic traditions. Ethiopia, ruled by Emperor Yohannes IV, was a Christian empire with roots stretching back centuries, capable of fielding armies that would prove, in time, to be more than a match for European infantry.

European contact with Africa was, of course, ancient. Portuguese sailors had rounded the Cape of Good Hope in 1488. The Atlantic slave trade had devastated West African societies for three centuries. Settlers of Dutch and later British descent had been farming the southern tip of the continent since the mid-seventeenth century. But in 1880, actual European territorial control of Africa was remarkably limited. The British held the Cape Colony and Natal. The French had Algeria in the north and a scattering of coastal trading posts in the west. The Portuguese clung to Angola and Mozambique, colonies more nominal than real in their interior extent. In total, Europeans formally controlled perhaps twenty percent of the continent — and much of that was coastal fringe, not the deep interior.

What changed? Several things converged in the late nineteenth century to make the interior suddenly accessible and, in the minds of European statesmen and investors, suddenly worth having. The steamship made river navigation faster and more reliable. Quinine, derived from cinchona bark, had been identified as a prophylactic against malaria — the disease that had killed so many European soldiers and traders that West Africa was nicknamed "the white man's grave." The breech-loading rifle and, later, the Maxim machine gun gave small European forces a decisive military edge over armies armed with older weapons. And the electric telegraph meant that orders could travel from London or Paris to a colonial governor faster than any horse could carry them.

Add to this the peculiar fever of the age: the late Victorian conviction, amplified by Social Darwinist theory and missionary zeal, that European civilization had not merely the right but the duty to expand. Commerce, Christianity, and civilization — the three C's of imperial ideology — were understood as gifts being bestowed upon a grateful or soon-to-be-grateful world. That this framing required the systematic erasure of existing African civilizations from the moral ledger was a contradiction that very few Europeans in positions of power chose to examine.

---

The Conference Itself

The Berlin Conference — formally, the West Africa Conference, or Kongokonferenz as the Germans called it — was convened by Otto von Bismarck and opened on November 15, 1884. Bismarck had his own reasons for hosting it. Germany was a newcomer to the imperial game, having only unified as a nation in 1871, and Bismarck wanted both to establish German colonial credibility and to use the conference as a diplomatic tool to manage Franco-British rivalry in a way that served German interests. If the great powers were squabbling over Africa, they were less likely to form coalitions against Germany in Europe.

The immediate trigger was the Congo. King Leopold II of Belgium — a man whose public persona was that of a philanthropic monarch and whose private reality was something far darker — had been quietly acquiring territory in the Congo Basin through his International Association of the Congo, a private venture he controlled personally. The French and the British both had competing claims and interests in the region. Someone needed to sort it out.

The conference lasted until February 26, 1885, and its formal agreements — the General Act of Berlin — established principles that would govern, in theory if not always in practice, the European partition of Africa for the next three decades. The key provisions were these: any European power wishing to claim African territory had to notify the other signatories; it had to demonstrate "effective occupation" of the territory it claimed; and free trade was to be guaranteed in the Congo Basin and along the Niger River.

What the conference did not do is as important as what it did. It did not draw the actual borders of Africa. That would happen piecemeal, through bilateral treaties, military expeditions, and diplomatic negotiations over the following fifteen years. It did not invite a single African representative to participate. And it did not, despite the rhetorical flourishes about humanitarian purpose and free trade, place any meaningful constraints on how European powers might treat the people living in the territories they claimed.

Leopold emerged from Berlin as the personal sovereign of the Congo Free State — a territory roughly the size of Western Europe that he owned as private property. What he proceeded to do there, in the name of rubber extraction and profit, would eventually shock even the cynical conscience of the imperial age. But that reckoning was still years away.

---

The Rush That Followed

The two decades after Berlin were the actual scramble — a headlong, often violent, sometimes farcical rush to plant flags before a rival could get there first. British officers marched north from the Cape. French columns pushed east from Senegal toward the upper Niger. German administrators carved out Tanganyika, Cameroon, and South-West Africa. The Italians tried and, at Adwa in 1896, spectacularly failed to take Ethiopia, suffering a defeat that remains one of the most decisive African military victories over a European invader in the entire colonial era.

The human cost was staggering and largely invisible to European publics. The Herero and Nama peoples of German South-West Africa were subjected to what historians now recognize as one of the first genocides of the twentieth century, beginning in 1904, when German colonial forces drove entire populations into the Omaheke Desert to die of thirst. In the Congo Free State, Leopold's rubber regime — enforced by the severing of hands as punishment for insufficient production — killed millions. Historians debate the numbers, but estimates of the death toll in the Congo between 1885 and 1908 range from one million to ten million people.

By 1914, on the eve of the First World War, Europeans controlled approximately ninety percent of the African continent. Only Ethiopia and Liberia remained formally independent. A process that had taken less than thirty years had remade the political geography of an entire continent.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Historians have argued for decades about what, precisely, drove the Scramble. The older "economic" explanation — advanced most famously by J.A. Hobson in 1902 and later adapted by Lenin — held that European capitalism, having saturated its home markets, needed new outlets for surplus capital and goods, and that Africa was the result. This view has been substantially complicated by subsequent research showing that, in strict economic terms, most of Africa was not particularly profitable for the colonizing powers, at least not immediately. The costs of administration often outweighed the returns.

Ronald Robinson and John Gallagher, in their influential 1961 work Africa and the Victorians, shifted the emphasis toward strategic and political calculations, arguing that Britain in particular was driven into the African interior not by economic appetite but by the need to protect the route to India and to respond to instability in Egypt after 1882. On this reading, the scramble was less a planned land-grab than a cascade of strategic reactions, each move by one power triggering defensive moves by others.

More recent scholarship, particularly from African historians and postcolonial theorists, has challenged the very framing of the debate — pointing out that centering the question on European motivations reproduces the conference's own erasure of African agency. Scholars like A. Adu Boahen have documented the sophisticated range of African responses to colonialism: armed resistance, diplomatic negotiation, selective accommodation, and cultural preservation. The scramble was not something that simply happened to Africa; it was contested at every step, even when the contestation ultimately failed.

---

The Aftermath

The colonial period that the Berlin Conference inaugurated lasted, in most of Africa, until the 1950s and 1960s, when a wave of independence movements — driven by African political organizing, the exhaustion of European powers after two World Wars, and the moral pressure of a changed international order — dismantled the formal structures of empire. But the borders drawn by European negotiators, often with no regard for existing ethnic, linguistic, or political boundaries, remained. The Organization of African Unity, founded in 1963, made the fateful decision to respect colonial borders rather than risk the chaos of reopening every territorial question simultaneously. It was a pragmatic choice with profound long-term consequences.

Those borders, lines drawn in Berlin and in subsequent bilateral treaties, run through the middle of ethnic homelands, separate communities that had historically been unified, and lump together peoples with no shared political tradition. They are not the only cause of the political instability, conflict, and underdevelopment that have afflicted parts of Africa since independence — the colonial period itself systematically extracted wealth, suppressed institutions, and distorted economies in ways that left deep structural damage — but they are a genuine contributing factor to tensions that continue to this day.

---

Why It Matters

The Berlin Conference is not merely a chapter in African history. It is a case study in how power, when sufficiently concentrated and sufficiently certain of its own righteousness, can redraw the world — and how the consequences of that redrawing outlast the confidence that produced it by generations.

It raises questions that have not gone away: Who has the right to draw lines on a map? What obligations do powerful states owe to weaker ones? How do we reckon with the gap between the humanitarian language that powerful actors use to justify their interventions and the material interests those interventions actually serve? The rhetoric of the Berlin Conference — civilization, free trade, the suppression of the slave trade — bears a family resemblance to the rhetoric that has accompanied many subsequent interventions in the affairs of the Global South.

The scramble also reminds us that the international order is not a natural fact but a constructed one, made by specific people at specific moments for specific reasons, and that its construction always involves choices about whose interests count and whose do not. The people who were not in that Berlin conference room in 1884 — the Zulu, the Sokoto, the Buganda, the Herero, the Congolese — were not absent because they had nothing to say. They were absent because the men at the table had decided, in advance, that what they might say did not matter.

That decision, and the world it made, is still very much with us.
