The City That Was Written in the Stars

How a Fatimid General, a Runaway Rope, and a Planet Named Mars Gave the World Cairo

---

The Daily Brief — Medieval History | North Africa

---

The Hook

The story goes that the astrologers were supposed to pull the rope.

It was 969 CE, and a Fatimid general named Jawhar al-Siqilli had just completed one of the most sweeping military campaigns in medieval history, rolling his army across the breadth of North Africa and into Egypt with barely a whisper of serious resistance. Now, on a flat plain just north of the old Arab garrison town of Fustat, his engineers had laid out the foundations of an entirely new city — a royal capital, an imperial statement, a place that would announce to the known world that a new dynasty had arrived and intended to stay. The boundaries of the city had been marked with poles and ropes hung with bells. When the astrologers identified the most auspicious celestial moment, they would ring the bells, the workers would begin turning the earth, and the city would be born under a lucky sky.

Then a crow landed on the rope.

The bells rang. The workers, trained to respond to that sound, drove their spades into the ground. And the astrologers looked up in horror, because the planet they had been waiting to set — Mars, called al-Qahir in Arabic, the Subduer, the Conqueror — had not yet risen. It was rising now, it was rising at precisely the wrong moment, and there was absolutely nothing to be done about it. The city would be named for that planet regardless. Al-Qahir. The Conqueror. Cairo.

Whether you believe this story as history or receive it as myth, it captures something true about the city that rose from that moment: Cairo was always going to be a place of enormous, barely-controlled power. The crow and the planet were simply the first signs.

---

The World Before

To understand what the Fatimids were building, you have to understand what they were building against.

By the middle of the tenth century, the Islamic world had fractured in ways that would have been unthinkable to the companions of the Prophet three centuries earlier. The Abbasid Caliphate, based in Baghdad, still commanded nominal spiritual authority over most of Sunni Islam, but it had become largely a ceremonial institution. Real power across the eastern Islamic world had passed to Buyid commanders, Turkic warlords, and ambitious regional governors who paid the caliph the minimum of respect required by political custom and otherwise did as they pleased. The caliphate was a name on a coin, a signature on a document.

Into this fracture stepped the Fatimids — and they were not merely another ambitious dynasty. They were Ismaili Shia Muslims, which meant they did not recognize the Abbasid caliphate as legitimate at all. In their theology, legitimate authority over the entire Muslim world belonged to the Imam, the descendant of the Prophet through his daughter Fatima and her husband Ali — hence Fatimid. They were not rebels against a system. They were the announcement that the system itself was wrong.

The dynasty had its roots in the Maghreb, in what is now Tunisia, where the Fatimid caliph al-Mahdi had established himself in 909 CE after a remarkable underground missionary network — the da'wa — had spent generations quietly building support across the Islamic world. From their capital at Mahdia on the Tunisian coast, the Fatimids had spent sixty years consolidating control over North Africa, fighting the Abbasids by proxy, and nursing a grand ambition: Egypt.

Egypt was the prize because Egypt was everything. It was the breadbasket of the Mediterranean, watered by the Nile in a way that made agriculture almost embarrassingly productive compared to the rest of the region. It sat at the crossroads of trade routes connecting the Mediterranean to the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean beyond. Whoever controlled Egypt controlled the flow of gold from sub-Saharan Africa, the spices from the East, the grain that fed cities from Alexandria to Palermo. The Fatimids wanted the world. Egypt was the door.

---

The Campaign and the Founding

In 969 CE, the Fatimid caliph al-Muizz li-Din Allah dispatched his greatest general, Jawhar, with an army variously estimated by medieval sources at somewhere between one hundred thousand and six hundred thousand men — numbers we should treat with considerable skepticism, though the army was certainly enormous by the standards of the age. Jawhar was himself a remarkable figure: a Sicilian-born former slave, likely of Greek or Slavic origin, who had risen through talent and loyalty to become the most trusted military commander in the Fatimid state. He was, in other words, the kind of person the medieval Islamic world produced in abundance and whom modern popular history tends to overlook — a man of no distinguished birth who bent the trajectory of empires.

The Ikhshidid dynasty that nominally controlled Egypt was in no condition to resist. Weakened by internal conflict and facing famine along the Nile, the Ikhshidids capitulated with almost indecent speed. Jawhar entered Fustat, the existing Arab capital just south of his planned city, in July 969 CE, and immediately began projecting an image of restraint and legitimacy. He promised safety to the population, protection to the existing religious communities — including the substantial Christian Coptic community and the Jewish community — and made it clear that the Fatimids had come not to plunder but to rule.

Then he began to build.

The new city was conceived from the outset as a royal enclosure — a place for the caliph, his court, his administration, and his army. It was not, at first, a city in the sense of a place where ordinary people lived and worked. It was closer to a fortified palace complex, a statement of sovereignty made in stone and brick. Jawhar oriented it carefully: to the south lay Fustat, which would continue to function as the commercial and residential heart of the region. To the north lay the older settlement of al-Askar and the even older Babylon fortress near the Nile. The new city sat between these layers of history like a crown placed atop them.

The caliph al-Muizz himself did not arrive until 973 CE, four years after the founding. He came from Mahdia with a procession that included the coffins of his Fatimid ancestors — a deliberate symbolic act, a declaration that the dynasty's center of gravity had permanently shifted eastward. When he arrived, the city was ready: its great mosque, the al-Azhar, had already been completed in 970 CE, barely a year after the founding. Al-Azhar — the radiant — was not merely a mosque. It was a university, a center of Ismaili theological instruction, a propaganda engine for the Fatimid vision of Islam. It would outlast the Fatimids by a thousand years and remains one of the most influential Islamic institutions in the world today.

---

The Scholarly Debate

The story of the crow and the bells is charming, but historians have spent considerable energy debating what it actually tells us — and whether it happened at all.

The account comes primarily from later sources, and like so much of medieval Islamic historiography, it exists in a complicated relationship with the events it describes. The Fatimids were nothing if not sophisticated propagandists, and the idea that their capital city was named for Mars, the planet of conquest and power, served their imperial narrative beautifully whether or not a crow was actually involved. Some scholars treat the story as a genuine folk memory of the founding ceremony, noting that the use of astrology to determine auspicious moments for construction was entirely standard practice in the medieval Islamic world and not at all the sort of thing that would need to be invented. Others suggest the Mars connection was a retrospective explanation, a way of making sense of a name whose origins may have been more prosaic.

There is also a broader debate about the nature of Fatimid rule in Egypt and what kind of city Cairo actually was in its early decades. The traditional account emphasizes the exclusivity of the royal enclosure — the idea that al-Qahira was a forbidden city, a palace-city closed to ordinary Egyptians, while Fustat remained the real urban center. More recent scholarship, including the work of historians like Paula Sanders and Paul Walker, has complicated this picture, suggesting that the boundaries between the royal city and the wider population were more permeable than the simple enclosure narrative implies, and that Cairo's character as a genuinely urban space developed earlier than previously thought.

There is also the question of how the Fatimids actually governed their Sunni majority population. The dynasty never made a serious attempt to convert Egypt to Ismaili Shia Islam. They ruled a largely Sunni population, maintained Sunni legal institutions, and tolerated — even patronized — religious diversity to a degree that was unusual for the period. Whether this represents genuine pluralism or simply pragmatic political calculation is a question that historians continue to argue, and the answer is probably that it was both, in the way that most successful imperial policies tend to be.

---

The Aftermath: What Rose from the Founding

Cairo under the Fatimids became something extraordinary: one of the wealthiest and most cosmopolitan cities on earth.

The Fatimid caliphs were legendary patrons of art, architecture, and scholarship. The treasury they accumulated was staggering — medieval sources describe storerooms containing tens of thousands of crystal vessels, mountains of gold and silver, libraries of unimaginable scope. When the dynasty eventually collapsed in financial crisis and civil conflict in the eleventh century, the dispersal of the Fatimid treasury sent luxury objects cascading across the Mediterranean world, some of which survive in European cathedral treasuries to this day, repurposed as Christian reliquaries by people who had no idea what they were looking at.

Al-Azhar became the intellectual center of the Fatimid world, drawing scholars, missionaries, and students from across the Islamic world and beyond. When the Fatimid dynasty ended — terminated by Saladin, the great Sunni general who took control of Egypt in 1171 CE and restored nominal Abbasid authority — al-Azhar was briefly sidelined, its Ismaili curriculum replaced. But the institution survived, transformed into a Sunni center of learning that has been continuously operating for over a thousand years.

Saladin himself recognized what the Fatimids had built. Rather than dismantling Cairo, he expanded it, incorporating Fustat and the surrounding settlements into a single great city and beginning construction of the Citadel that still dominates Cairo's skyline. The city the Fatimids had founded as a royal enclosure became, under subsequent dynasties — the Ayyubids, the Mamluks, the Ottomans — the largest city in the Mediterranean world and arguably in the entire world west of China. Ibn Battuta, visiting in the fourteenth century, described it with barely contained awe: a city so vast, so teeming, so inexhaustible in its energy that it seemed to him like the center of the universe.

That reputation has never entirely faded.

---

Why It Matters

The founding of Cairo is a story about several things that remain urgently relevant.

It is a story about how cities are made — not by accident or gradual accumulation alone, but sometimes by deliberate political will, by a dynasty that decides it needs a capital worthy of its ambitions and simply builds one. The history of the world is full of such founded cities, from Constantinople to Brasília, and they raise the same questions every time: What is a capital city for? Who does it serve? What relationship does it have with the population that surrounds it?

It is a story about religious pluralism under pressure. The Fatimids governed a religiously diverse society — Ismaili rulers, Sunni majority, Coptic Christians, Jews — and did so with a pragmatism that produced, at its best, genuine cultural flowering. The limits of that pluralism were real, and the Fatimids were not above using religion as a political weapon when it suited them. But the basic fact of their governance — that a minority sect could rule a majority population through competence, legitimacy, and strategic tolerance rather than forced conversion — is a model that has appeared in many forms throughout history and whose lessons have not been exhausted.

It is also a story about the persistence of institutions. Al-Azhar was founded in 970 CE. It is still there. It is still one of the most important centers of Islamic scholarship in the world. The people who built it were trying to propagate a specific theological vision that was eventually defeated and marginalized. The institution they created outlived their vision by a millennium and became something they would not entirely have recognized — which is to say, it became genuinely historic, shaped by forces far beyond its founders' intentions.

And it is a story about names. Al-Qahir. The Conqueror. A crow landed on a rope, a planet rose at the wrong moment, and a city received a name that has defined it ever since. There is something in that accident — or that myth — that captures the way great things come into being: through calculation and ambition, yes, but also through the unpredictable intrusion of the world as it actually is. The astrologers planned for a lucky star. They got Mars. And Mars, as it turned out, was exactly right.
