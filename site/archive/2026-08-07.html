The Daily Brief

When the World Cracked Open: The Russian Revolution of 1917

---

Issue date: Today | Era: Modern | Region: Europe

---

The Hook

Imagine standing in a bread line in Petrograd in February 1917. The temperature is well below freezing. You have been waiting for three hours. The bakery, when you finally reach its door, has nothing left to sell. Behind you, hundreds of other women — it is mostly women, because the men are dying in the trenches — shuffle their feet and breathe clouds of vapor into the grey morning air. The war has now consumed three years, three million Russian dead, and whatever faith you once had in the Tsar who sent them. You are hungry enough, cold enough, and tired enough that today, for the first time, you do not go home quietly. You go into the street instead. And it turns out that everyone else has made the same decision at the same moment.

That is how empires end. Not always with a cannon shot or a manifesto, but with a bread line that finally runs out of bread.

---

The World Before

To understand 1917, you have to understand what Russia was — and what it was trying, and failing, to become.

By the early twentieth century, the Russian Empire was the largest contiguous land empire in human history, stretching from Warsaw in the west to Vladivostok in the east, encompassing over 125 million people who spoke dozens of languages and practiced half a dozen religions. At its head sat Tsar Nicholas II, a man of genuine personal kindness and catastrophic political judgment, who believed with sincere conviction that God had appointed him to rule, that autocracy was the only form of government suited to Russia's nature, and that any concession to democratic reform was, at its root, a betrayal of divine will.

The empire he governed was modernizing with dizzying speed in some ways — railways threading across Siberia, factories rising in Moscow and Petrograd, a restless intelligentsia reading Marx and Spencer and Tolstoy — and frozen in medieval amber in others. Roughly eighty percent of the population were peasants, many of them still working land under conditions barely distinguishable from the serfdom abolished just two generations earlier in 1861. The emancipation had freed them legally but left them economically shackled, obligated to pay redemption dues for land they considered theirs by ancestral right.

There had already been one revolution, of a kind. In 1905, following a humiliating naval defeat by Japan and a massacre of peaceful petitioners outside the Winter Palace on what became known as Bloody Sunday, strikes and uprisings had forced Nicholas to issue the October Manifesto, promising a constitution and a parliament — the Duma. He had then spent the following decade systematically hollowing out everything he had promised. The Duma met, argued, and was periodically dissolved when it became inconvenient. The lesson the radical left drew from 1905 was not that reform was possible, but that the Tsar could not be trusted to deliver it.

Then came the First World War, which broke everything.

---

The Story

Russia entered the war in August 1914 in a blaze of patriotic feeling that dissolved with remarkable swiftness into suffering on an almost incomprehensible scale. The Russian army was enormous and often brave, but it was chronically undersupplied — soldiers were sometimes sent to the front without rifles, told to pick up weapons from the fallen. By 1917, military casualties numbered somewhere between 1.6 and 1.8 million dead, with millions more wounded or taken prisoner. The home front was collapsing under the weight of wartime requisitions, inflation, and a transport system so strained that food rotted in one province while people starved in another.

Nicholas, in one of his most consequential blunders, had assumed personal command of the army in 1915. This meant that every subsequent defeat was personally his. It also meant he was absent from the capital for months at a time, leaving the government in the hands of his wife Alexandra — who was, in turn, under the extraordinary influence of Grigori Rasputin, a Siberian mystic whose apparent ability to ease the suffering of their hemophiliac son Alexei had given him an almost supernatural hold over the royal family. The spectacle of a dissolute peasant healer effectively advising on ministerial appointments horrified the aristocracy and inflamed the popular imagination. By late 1916, even conservatives in the Duma were warning that the monarchy was destroying itself.

The February Revolution — which actually occurred in late February by the old Julian calendar Russia still used, placing it in early March by the Gregorian calendar the rest of Europe had long since adopted — began without any central organization or leadership. It grew from those bread lines. Women textile workers in the Vyborg district of Petrograd walked off their jobs on International Women's Day, February 23rd (Old Style), and spilled into the streets calling for bread. Workers from other factories joined them. By the following day, roughly 200,000 workers were on strike across the city. The Tsar, at military headquarters in Mogilev, ordered the garrison to fire on the crowds. Some units obeyed. Then, on February 27th, the soldiers of the Pavlovsky Regiment refused to shoot at the demonstrators and turned their rifles on their own officers instead. One by one, other regiments followed. The revolution was made not by professional revolutionaries but by hungry women and mutinous conscripts.

Nicholas abdicated on March 2nd. He tried, with a strange detached sadness his diary records with heartbreaking understatement, to pass the throne to his brother Michael. Michael declined. After three hundred years, the Romanov dynasty simply ceased.

What followed was a peculiar, unstable duality that would define the next eight months. A Provisional Government formed from Duma liberals and moderate socialists, led first by Prince Lvov and then by the charismatic lawyer Alexander Kerensky, claimed state authority. But simultaneously, soviets — councils of workers and soldiers — sprang up across the country, and the Petrograd Soviet held the real loyalty of the garrison and the factories. These two bodies shared power awkwardly, each tolerating the other, neither quite able to act without the other's consent.

The Provisional Government made a decision that, in retrospect, looks like a slow-motion suicide: it chose to continue the war. Partly this was allied pressure, partly liberal principle about honoring treaty commitments, partly Kerensky's own romantic nationalism. But the soldiers who had just mutinied to end the killing were not interested in liberal principles. The army was dissolving, men walking home from the front in their hundreds of thousands.

Into this volatile situation, in April 1917, the Germans made one of history's most consequential gambles. They transported Vladimir Ilyich Lenin — the exiled leader of the Bolshevik faction of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party, who had been sitting out the war in Zurich — across Germany in a sealed train, calculating that he would accelerate Russia's collapse and remove her from the war. They were correct, though the consequences they unleashed would haunt the world for a century.

Lenin arrived at Petrograd's Finland Station on the night of April 3rd to a crowd of thousands and immediately delivered a speech that stunned even his own followers. While other socialist parties were cautiously supporting the Provisional Government, Lenin called for its immediate overthrow, an end to the war, and the transfer of all power to the soviets. His "April Theses" were so radical that even senior Bolsheviks initially dismissed them. But they captured the exhausted, furious mood of the streets with uncanny precision.

Through the summer of 1917, the Bolsheviks grew with extraordinary speed, their message — Peace, Land, Bread — a perfect compression of everything the population desperately wanted. A failed military offensive in July, a right-wing coup attempt by General Kornilov in August that Kerensky had to arm the soviets to defeat, a spiraling economic crisis — each crisis pushed more soldiers, workers, and peasants toward the Bolsheviks. By October, the party controlled the Petrograd and Moscow soviets.

The October Revolution — October 25th by the old calendar, November 7th by the new — was almost anticlimactic compared to the elemental fury of February. It was, in large part, a coup. The Bolsheviks' Military Revolutionary Committee, organized by Leon Trotsky with cold tactical efficiency, seized key infrastructure — the telegraph, the railway stations, the bridges, the power stations — in the early hours of October 25th. The Winter Palace, seat of the Provisional Government, was stormed by a Red Guard and a naval crew late that night, the defense offered by a small contingent of officer cadets and a Women's Battalion. Kerensky had already fled. The ministers were arrested. The Bolsheviks announced that power had passed to the soviets.

John Reed, the American journalist who witnessed it all, described the scene in the Smolny Institute — the Bolshevik headquarters — as Lenin appeared before the Congress of Soviets the following morning to announce the new government: "Now we shall build the socialist order." The hall, Reed wrote, erupted in a roar that seemed to shake the building.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Historians have argued for over a century about what, exactly, the Russian Revolution was. Was it a genuine popular uprising, an expression of deep social forces that made some kind of revolution inevitable? Or was it, particularly in October, essentially a Bolshevik coup that hijacked a more organic democratic revolution? The liberal historian Richard Pipes argued forcefully for the latter view, portraying the Bolsheviks as a conspiratorial minority who seized power over the wishes of a majority that wanted democracy, not socialist dictatorship. Revisionist social historians like Sheila Fitzpatrick and Ronald Suny have pushed back, emphasizing the genuine radicalism of workers and soldiers, the real popular base of Bolshevik support in the autumn of 1917.

There is also the counterfactual question that haunts the period: was Stalin's terror an inevitable outgrowth of Leninism, or was it a contingent catastrophe that a different set of circumstances might have avoided? Orlando Figes, in his monumental A People's Tragedy, suggests that the seeds of totalitarianism were present in Bolshevik ideology from the beginning. Others point to the genuine, if brief, pluralism of the early Soviet period as evidence that history could have gone differently.

What is beyond serious dispute is that the revolution was not made by the Bolsheviks alone. It was made by millions of desperate, suffering people whose aspirations the Bolsheviks then captured — and ultimately betrayed.

---

The Aftermath

The Bolsheviks consolidated power with terrifying speed and violence. A civil war between the Red Army and a loose coalition of White armies, foreign interventionists, nationalist movements, and anarchists consumed the former empire from 1918 to 1921, killing millions more through combat, famine, and epidemic. The Red Terror — the systematic use of mass execution and hostage-taking against class enemies — established the template for Soviet political violence. The Cheka, the secret police founded in December 1917, would evolve through multiple names and forms into the KGB.

The Romanovs were shot in a basement in Yekaterinburg in July 1918 — Nicholas, Alexandra, their five children, and their servants — on Lenin's orders, or at minimum with his approval.

From the rubble of the civil war emerged the Soviet Union, formally constituted in 1922. It would endure for sixty-nine years, reshape the global order, inspire revolutionary movements on every continent, fight the largest land war in human history, and develop nuclear weapons capable of ending civilization. It would also, in the process of forced collectivization and industrialization under Stalin, kill somewhere between six and twenty million of its own citizens — a range whose imprecision reflects both the horror of the scale and the ongoing difficulty of the historical accounting.

---

Why It Matters

The Russian Revolution of 1917 is not a story about Russia alone. It is a story about what happens when states lose legitimacy — when the gap between what a government promises and what it delivers becomes wide enough that people decide, collectively and almost simultaneously, that the cost of rebellion is lower than the cost of endurance.

It is a story about the seductive power of radical simplicity — Peace, Land, Bread — in a moment of complex, grinding crisis. And about how revolutionary movements that begin with genuine popular energy can be captured by disciplined minorities with clear organizational structures and the willingness to use violence.

It is a story about the consequences of war. Every modern authoritarian revolution of the twentieth century — in Russia, Italy, Germany, China — grew from the wreckage of a military catastrophe. War does not only kill soldiers. It kills the political arrangements that sent them to die.

And it is, finally, a story about the durable human tension between the desire for order and the hunger for justice — and what happens in the terrible moments when a society discovers it can no longer have both.

The bread lines are still out there, in different cities, in different forms. They have always been the place where history decides to change its mind.
