The Daily Brief

The Day the Sky Fell on Santa Fe: The Pueblo Revolt of 1680

August 10, 1680. Before dawn, a runner arrives too late.

---

The Hook

The Spanish governor of New Mexico, Antonio de Otermín, was still rubbing sleep from his eyes when the messenger reached the Palace of the Governors in Santa Fe. The news came in fragments, each worse than the last: the Pueblo peoples had risen. The two Tewa runners he had sent to warn the northern villages had been captured. The revolt, planned for the following day, had been moved up by a single night. Somewhere out in the high desert darkness, thousands of people who had endured eighty-two years of colonial rule were already in motion, moving toward missions and estancias with fire and purpose. By the time the sun cleared the Sandia Mountains that morning, the colonial world the Spanish had built along the Rio Grande was already coming apart at the seams.

What happened next was the most successful Indigenous uprising in the history of North America — a revolution so thorough that it drove the Spanish entirely out of New Mexico for twelve years, killed over four hundred colonists, and forced the most powerful empire in the Western Hemisphere to reconsider, fundamentally, how it governed the people it had conquered.

---

The World Before

To understand what exploded in August of 1680, you have to understand what the Rio Grande valley looked like in the century before it.

The Pueblo peoples — Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, Zuni, Hopi, and others — had been living in the high desert of present-day New Mexico for centuries before any European set foot on the continent. Their towns, some of them multi-storied stone and adobe structures perched on mesas or nestled beside rivers, had been home to sophisticated agricultural societies for generations. They grew corn, beans, and squash in a landscape that demanded extraordinary ingenuity with water. They traded across vast networks. They maintained complex ceremonial lives rooted in kachina religion, in the rhythms of planting and rain, in the sacred geography of a landscape they had made their own over hundreds of years.

The Spanish arrived in force in 1598, when Juan de Oñate led a colonizing expedition north from Mexico and established the province of Nuevo México. The encounter was catastrophic for the Pueblos almost immediately. When the people of Acoma Pueblo resisted Spanish demands for food and supplies — demands that amounted to confiscation — Oñate's response was annihilating. His soldiers killed hundreds of Acoma's inhabitants, enslaved survivors, and cut off the right foot of every adult male prisoner. It was a message written in blood, and it set the tone for the colonial relationship that followed.

What followed was the encomienda system, a structure in which Spanish colonists were granted the labor of Pueblo people as a kind of tribute. Pueblo men and women worked Spanish fields, herded Spanish livestock, and built Spanish missions — the great adobe churches that still stand, or whose ruins still stand, across New Mexico today. The Franciscan friars who came with the colonists were zealous in their mission: they suppressed Pueblo religious practices, smashed kachina masks and prayer sticks, and in 1675 ordered the arrest of forty-seven Pueblo religious leaders on charges of witchcraft. Three were hanged. The rest were publicly flogged.

One of the men flogged that day was a Tewa man from Ohkay Owingeh (San Juan Pueblo) named Popé.

The Spanish colonial economy also placed Pueblo people in an impossible position regarding the environment. The settlers' demands for labor and food came during a period of severe drought and intensifying raids from Apache and Navajo peoples, who were themselves being displaced and pressured by the expanding colonial system. The Pueblos were being squeezed from every direction — by Spanish tribute demands, by mission labor, by crop failures, by raids — and the Spanish colonial apparatus showed no willingness to relieve any of it.

By the 1670s, the Pueblo world was under existential pressure. Something had to give.

---

The Conspiracy and the Rising

Popé had survived his flogging. He had not forgiven it.

After 1675, he withdrew to Taos Pueblo, far to the north, where he began organizing what would become one of the most carefully coordinated uprisings in colonial American history. The conspiracy was remarkable not just for its ambition but for the logistical problem it solved: it brought together people from dozens of distinct Pueblo communities, speaking several different languages, with their own local histories and grievances, into a single coordinated action. Popé reportedly told the Pueblo leaders that he had received instruction from the kachinas — specifically from three spirits named Caudi, Tilini, and Tleume — who promised that if the Pueblos drove out the Spanish and returned to the old ways, the rains would return, the crops would flourish, and the Apache raids would cease.

The plan was set for August 11, 1680. The signal would be carried by runners bearing knotted cords — one knot to be untied each day until the day of the rising. But on August 9th, two men from Tesuque Pueblo, Catua and Omtua, revealed the conspiracy to the Spanish. Popé learned of the betrayal almost immediately and made the decision that would define the revolt: he moved the date up to August 10th, before the Spanish could mount a coordinated response.

In the predawn hours of August 10th, the Pueblo warriors struck. The Franciscan friars, isolated in their missions across the province, were among the first to die — twenty-one of the thirty-three priests in New Mexico were killed that day and the days that followed. Settlers on outlying farms and ranches were attacked. The violence was deliberately comprehensive. Popé had instructed the Pueblo people to destroy every trace of Spanish colonialism: to burn the churches, kill the livestock the Spanish had brought, throw the baptismal records into the river, and wash the marks of Christian baptism from their own bodies.

Governor Otermín, barricaded in Santa Fe with a few hundred survivors, held out for several days as Pueblo warriors surrounded the capital. The warriors cut off the town's water supply — the acequia that fed the Palace — and Otermín, wounded twice by arrows, eventually led his surviving colonists in a desperate breakout to the south. The Spanish fled down the Rio Grande, abandoning Santa Fe, abandoning their missions, abandoning their province entirely. By late August, Otermín and roughly two thousand survivors — colonists and Pueblo people who had remained loyal to the Spanish — had retreated to the area near present-day El Paso, Texas. New Mexico was free.

---

The Scholarly Debate

History is never as clean as its heroes, and Popé is no exception.

For much of the twentieth century, the Pueblo Revolt was narrated largely from Spanish sources — Otermín's reports, the depositions taken from Pueblo prisoners after the Spanish reconquest — and this gave historians a particular kind of picture: Popé as a charismatic, almost authoritarian figure who imposed unity through fear, who executed men he suspected of disloyalty (including his own son-in-law, Catua, one of the men who had revealed the plot). Some accounts portray him as increasingly despotic in the years after the revolt, demanding tribute from the Pueblos in ways that echoed the very colonial system he had overthrown.

Andrew Knaut, Joe Sando, Andrew Isbell, and others who have worked more carefully with oral traditions and Pueblo-centered perspectives have complicated this picture considerably. The revolt was not simply Popé's achievement — it was a collective action by dozens of communities, each with their own leaders and motivations, and the coordination required trust as much as coercion. The Spanish-derived accounts of Popé's post-revolt tyranny may reflect Spanish attempts to delegitimize the revolution after the fact, or may reflect the genuine difficulty of governing a coalition that had been united by a common enemy and then had to figure out what came next.

There is also the question of religion. The revolt is sometimes framed purely as a political uprising against colonial exploitation, and sometimes as a religious revitalization movement — and it was both, inseparably. Popé's instructions to wash away baptism and return to the kachina religion were not merely symbolic. They were a claim about the nature of the world: that the Spanish God had not brought rain or protection, that the Spanish God was a lie, and that the return to Pueblo ceremonial life was the only path back to balance. Whether that claim was "sincere" religious belief or political strategy is perhaps a false distinction, but historians continue to argue about the relative weight of material grievance and spiritual motivation.

---

The Aftermath

The Spanish did not stay away forever. In 1692, Governor Diego de Vargas led a reconquest of New Mexico, retaking Santa Fe with relatively little bloodshed — a fact that has led to considerable debate about what "reconquest" actually meant in practice. The Pueblos rose again in 1696, and that revolt was suppressed more violently.

But the world after the revolt was not the same as the world before it. The Spanish returned to New Mexico changed. The encomienda system was not reimposed. Forced labor demands were reduced significantly. The Franciscan friars continued their missionary work, but with considerably less appetite for destroying kachina masks and flogging medicine men. The Spanish, in short, had learned something: that there were limits to what colonial subjects would endure, and that those limits had consequences.

The Pueblo Revolt also had effects that rippled far beyond New Mexico. When the Spanish fled south in 1680, they left behind horses — thousands of horses. Those horses were acquired by Apache and Navajo groups, who traded and raided them further north and east, transforming the cultures of the Great Plains over the following decades. The horse revolution that made the Plains Indian cultures of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries possible — the buffalo-hunting, the warfare, the mobility — was accelerated dramatically by the horses that fled south from Santa Fe in August of 1680. In this sense, the Pueblo Revolt reshaped the history of a continent.

Popé himself died around 1692, before the Spanish returned, the timing uncertain in the sources. He did not live to see the reconquest, and what he made of the years after the revolt — the drought that continued, the inter-Pueblo tensions, the Apache raids that did not cease — we cannot know with certainty. He remains one of the most significant and least widely known figures in North American history.

---

Why It Matters

The Pueblo Revolt sits at the intersection of several of the most durable questions in American history: about the nature of colonial power, about the limits of what empire can impose on the people it claims to govern, about the relationship between religious identity and political resistance, and about how the colonized find ways to act collectively across lines of language and community that colonizers assume will keep them divided.

It matters, too, as a corrective to a certain kind of historical amnesia. The standard narrative of early American history tends to begin on the Atlantic coast, with English colonists and their struggles. The Pueblo Revolt reminds us that the history of North America in the seventeenth century was happening across a vast continent simultaneously, and that the people who lived here before the Europeans arrived were not passive recipients of history but active makers of it — capable of extraordinary strategic thinking, capable of sacrifice, capable of winning.

And it matters as a story about what happens when a governing system refuses to listen to the people it governs until those people have no other option. The Spanish in New Mexico had eighty-two years of warning signs. They flogged the wrong man in 1675. The cost of that decision was paid on August 10, 1680.

The reckoning, when it comes, rarely announces itself in advance.
