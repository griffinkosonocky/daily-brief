The Daily Brief

When Empires Devour Their Own: The Armenian Genocide and the Collapse of Ottoman Power

Modern Era · West Asia

---

The Hook

In the early hours of April 24, 1915, Ottoman police fanned out across Constantinople and arrested approximately 235 Armenian intellectuals, doctors, clergy, and community leaders. They were loaded onto trains and taken into the Anatolian interior. Most were never seen again. That single night — now commemorated annually as Armenian Genocide Remembrance Day — was not the beginning of the violence, nor even its worst chapter. But it announced something to those who understood the language of authoritarian states: a society was being prepared to accept the erasure of a people. What followed was one of the twentieth century's first systematic campaigns of mass extermination, carried out in the dying convulsions of an empire that had ruled for six centuries, and it would leave wounds in the landscape of history that have never fully healed.

---

The World Before

To understand what happened to the Armenians, you have to understand what the Ottoman Empire had become by the early twentieth century — and what it feared it was becoming.

For centuries, the Ottomans had governed a vast, multiethnic, multireligious empire stretching from the Balkans to the Persian Gulf, from Egypt to the Caucasus. Christians, Jews, and Muslims lived under a system called the millet, in which non-Muslim communities were granted a degree of religious autonomy in exchange for loyalty and a special tax. It was never an egalitarian arrangement — non-Muslims occupied a subordinate legal status — but it was, in its way, a functioning pluralism. Armenians, who had inhabited the highlands of eastern Anatolia for millennia long before any Turk arrived from Central Asia, built churches, ran schools, dominated certain trades, and produced poets and merchants and priests who were woven into the fabric of Ottoman urban life.

But the nineteenth century had not been kind to the empire. A string of military defeats had stripped away territory with humiliating regularity. Greece, Serbia, Romania, Bulgaria — one by one, Christian minorities in the Balkans had broken free, often with the encouragement of European great powers who spoke the language of nationalism and self-determination when it was convenient for them. The Ottomans watched their empire shrink and grew increasingly paranoid about which minority group might be the next to demand independence, the next to invite foreign intervention.

The Armenians were, in this calculus, particularly suspect. They were Christian. They lived in the eastern provinces near the Russian border — and Russia, the Ottoman Empire's perennial enemy, had its own Armenian population and occasionally posed as their protector. When Armenian reformers in the 1890s began agitating for better treatment and greater autonomy, Sultan Abdul Hamid II responded with massacres that killed somewhere between 80,000 and 300,000 Armenians between 1894 and 1896. The world watched, issued protests, and did very little. The lesson was not lost.

By 1908, a revolutionary movement called the Committee of Union and Progress — known in the West as the Young Turks — had seized effective power from the sultan, promising modernization, constitutional government, and equality for all Ottoman citizens regardless of religion. Many Armenians initially celebrated. The hope lasted only a few years. As the empire suffered further catastrophic losses — the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913 stripped away nearly all of its remaining European territory — the Young Turk leadership, dominated by a triumvirate known as the Three Pashas (Enver, Talat, and Cemal), hardened into a narrow ethnonationalist ideology. The empire would survive, they decided, but it would survive as a Turkish empire. The pluralism that had once been its defining feature would be its undoing.

Then came the First World War.

---

The Story

When the Ottomans entered the war in November 1914 on the side of Germany and Austria-Hungary, Enver Pasha — the vain, ambitious Minister of War — launched a disastrous winter offensive against Russian forces in the Caucasus. At the Battle of Sarikamish in December 1914 and January 1915, the Ottoman Third Army was virtually destroyed, losing perhaps 90,000 men to Russian firepower and the savage cold. Enver needed an explanation that did not implicate his own catastrophically bad generalship. He found one: Armenian treachery. Some Armenians had indeed fought on the Russian side — many were Russian subjects, after all — and there had been scattered local Armenian uprisings in response to Ottoman brutality. This was inflated, in the telling of the Young Turk leadership, into a vast conspiracy of fifth-column betrayal.

The deportation orders began in the spring of 1915. On the surface, they were framed as a military security measure — Armenian populations in the eastern provinces were to be relocated to the Syrian desert, away from the front lines. But the implementation revealed the true intent almost immediately. Armenian men of fighting age were typically separated from the columns and shot. Women, children, and the elderly were driven into the desert on foot with little food or water, under the guard of Ottoman gendarmes and irregular forces — many of them Kurdish tribal fighters who had been armed and encouraged by the government, though it should be noted that some Kurdish leaders also sheltered Armenians at great personal risk.

The routes became death marches. Survivors' accounts — collected by American diplomats, German military officers, and missionaries in real time — describe scenes of almost incomprehensible cruelty: mass drownings in the Euphrates, burnings in stables and churches, bodies left to line the roads for miles. The concentration point for many deportees was the Syrian desert around Deir ez-Zor, where tens of thousands more died of starvation and disease, or were killed outright when even the pretext of relocation was abandoned.

Henry Morgenthau Sr., the American ambassador to Constantinople, met repeatedly with Talat Pasha and recorded those conversations with a diplomat's precision and a human being's horror. When Morgenthau pressed Talat to stop the killings, Talat reportedly told him: "We have already disposed of three-quarters of the Armenians in Turkey." He then, with extraordinary brazenness, asked Morgenthau to use his contacts with American life insurance companies to obtain a list of Armenian policyholders — since, as Talat explained, those Armenians were now dead and the government was their heir.

The killing was not uniform across the empire. Armenians in Constantinople and Smyrna were somewhat more protected, partly because of the presence of foreign observers. In some regions, local Ottoman officials refused to carry out the orders and saved thousands. But in the eastern provinces — in Bitlis, Van, Erzurum, Diyarbakir — the destruction was nearly total. Communities that had existed for centuries were obliterated within months.

By the time the violence had run its course, somewhere between 600,000 and 1.5 million Armenians were dead. The range reflects not uncertainty about whether the genocide occurred, but the genuine difficulty of counting the dead in a region convulsed by war, famine, and deliberate record destruction. Perhaps 300,000 to 500,000 survived by escaping into the Russian Caucasus or the Arab provinces, or by hiding with neighbors who chose humanity over compliance.

---

The Scholarly Debate

The historical debate about the Armenian Genocide is, in one sense, not really a debate at all among scholars. The overwhelming consensus of genocide researchers, historians of the Ottoman Empire, and international bodies is that what occurred was a deliberate, state-organized campaign of extermination — that the word genocide, coined by Raphael Lemkin in 1944 partly with the Armenian case in mind, applies with precision. The documentation is enormous: Ottoman internal telegrams, German military and diplomatic dispatches, American consular reports, survivor testimony, demographic data.

The "debate," such as it is, has been conducted primarily by the Turkish state, which for over a century has maintained that the deaths — while regrettable — resulted from wartime chaos, disease, famine, and mutual intercommunal violence rather than a centralized extermination policy. This position has been backed by significant diplomatic and financial resources, and it has had real consequences: the United States, for reasons connected to its alliance with Turkey through NATO, did not formally recognize the genocide until 2021, when President Biden became the first American president to use the word officially.

Genuine scholarly complexity does exist around the edges. Historians debate the precise degree of premeditation — whether extermination was the plan from the start or whether the policy radicalized during implementation, as bureaucratic momentum and wartime brutality fed on each other. There are questions about the role of Kurdish irregular forces and how much agency local actors had versus how much they were directed from Constantinople. There are debates about the fate of Armenians who converted to Islam — some survived; others were killed anyway. These are real and important questions. They do not disturb the central finding.

What makes the Armenian Genocide particularly instructive for the history of atrocity is precisely how well-documented it was, even in real time, and how little that documentation moved the great powers to act. The phrase "who, after all, speaks today of the annihilation of the Armenians?" was reportedly spoken by Adolf Hitler in August 1939, on the eve of the invasion of Poland. Whether he said it in exactly those words is disputed — but the sentiment was accurate. The world had watched, recorded, and largely moved on.

---

The Aftermath and What Rose From It

The Ottoman Empire did not long outlast its crime. Defeated in the First World War, it was carved up by the victorious Allies under the Treaty of Sèvres in 1920, which briefly envisioned an independent Armenian homeland in eastern Anatolia. But the nationalist movement led by Mustafa Kemal — Atatürk — fought a successful war of independence, the Allies retreated from their commitments, and the new Republic of Turkey emerged in 1923 with no Armenian homeland and no accountability for the genocide. The Treaty of Lausanne replaced Sèvres. The great powers, exhausted and distracted, accepted the outcome.

The survivors scattered into a diaspora that eventually established communities in Lebanon, Syria, France, Argentina, and the United States — particularly in Los Angeles and Fresno, California. They carried with them a collective memory of catastrophic loss and an identity forged in trauma. The Armenian Apostolic Church became a center of diasporic life, as did a fierce attachment to the question of recognition that would define Armenian political culture for the rest of the century.

In Soviet Armenia — a small rump of the historical Armenian homeland that became part of the USSR — the genocide was acknowledged, then suppressed, then acknowledged again depending on Soviet political needs. After the collapse of the Soviet Union, the Republic of Armenia emerged as an independent state in 1991, with Yerevan as its capital, but without the historic heartland of western Armenia, which remains part of Turkey.

The question of recognition has slowly, unevenly, moved forward. France, Germany, Canada, and dozens of other countries have formally recognized the genocide. The International Association of Genocide Scholars has done so definitively. But Turkey's denial remains official policy, and the relationship between Turkey and Armenia — which share a border that has been closed since 1993, in part due to a separate conflict over Nagorno-Karabakh — remains one of the most poisoned bilateral relationships in the region.

---

Why It Matters

The Armenian Genocide sits at the intersection of several of the most durable and troubling themes in modern history, and that is precisely why it continues to demand attention from anyone trying to understand the world we live in.

It is, first, a case study in how empires die — not quietly, not gracefully, but often by turning their accumulated violence inward. The Ottoman Empire, faced with the humiliation of territorial loss and the existential anxiety of nationalist dissolution, chose to define itself by exclusion rather than inclusion, and the most vulnerable paid the price. This pattern — the collapsing empire that scapegoats a minority — is not unique to the Ottomans, and it did not end in 1915.

It is also a case study in the failure of international response. The documentation was there. The witnesses were there. The ambassadors filed their cables, the missionaries wrote their accounts, the photographs were taken. And the machinery of great-power interest ground on regardless. The lesson that atrocity can be documented in real time and still go unanswered is one that the twentieth century would teach again and again — in the Holocaust, in Cambodia, in Rwanda, in Bosnia. Each time, the world expressed shock. Each time, the precedent of inaction was already there.

And it is, finally, a case study in the politics of memory. The century-long struggle for recognition — the way that acknowledging a historical crime becomes entangled with contemporary alliance structures, diplomatic convenience, and national identity — tells us something important about how history actually works. The past is not fixed. It is contested, suppressed, weaponized, and occasionally, slowly, honestly reckoned with. The Armenians who marched into the Syrian desert in 1915 could not have imagined that their grandchildren would still be fighting for the word genocide to be spoken by foreign heads of state. But that fight is also a form of survival, and survival — against very long odds — is what the Armenian story has always been about.
