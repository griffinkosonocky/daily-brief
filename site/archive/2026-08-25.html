The Long Reconquest: How Christian Kingdoms Remade the Iberian Peninsula

The Daily Brief — Medieval History

---

A Kingdom Balanced on a Knife's Edge

Imagine standing on the ramparts of a small stone fortress somewhere in the mountains of Asturias, in the far northwest corner of the Iberian Peninsula, sometime around the year 740. Below you, the valleys are quiet. But the quiet is deceptive. To the south, stretching all the way to the straits that separate Europe from Africa, a new civilization has planted itself with breathtaking speed — Arabic-speaking, Muslim, administratively sophisticated, and militarily formidable. It calls itself Al-Andalus, and it has swallowed nearly everything that was once Visigothic Hispania in fewer than three decades.

You are, in this imagining, one of a dwindling company of Christian nobles, perhaps loyal to a chieftain named Pelayo, clinging to a strip of mountainous territory that the Muslim governors to the south largely consider not worth the trouble of subduing. Your kingdom is tiny. Your resources are thin. The idea that your descendants — or their descendants, or theirs — would one day drive the last Muslim ruler from the peninsula and plant a cross over Granada would strike you as something between a prophecy and a madman's dream.

And yet, that is exactly what happened. Over roughly seven and a half centuries, in fits and starts, through alliance and treachery, through plague and crusade and dynastic accident, the patchwork of Christian kingdoms in northern Iberia expanded southward until, in January 1492, the Nasrid sultan Boabdil surrendered the keys of Granada to Ferdinand and Isabella. The process historians call the Reconquista — the Reconquest — is one of the longest, most complicated, and most consequential military and cultural transformations in European history.

---

The World Before: Al-Andalus in Its Prime

To understand what the Christian kingdoms were pushing against, you have to appreciate what Al-Andalus actually was — and resist the temptation to see it simply as an obstacle to some inevitable Christian triumph.

When Muslim armies crossed the Strait of Gibraltar in 711 under the Berber general Tariq ibn Ziyad, they encountered a Visigothic kingdom already fractured by internal rivalry. The conquest was startlingly rapid. By 718 or so, the peninsula was largely under Muslim control, save for those northern mountain redoubts. What followed was not merely a military occupation but a profound cultural transplanting. Córdoba, the capital of the Umayyad emirate (and later caliphate), grew into one of the most sophisticated cities in the medieval world. By the tenth century, it may have been home to half a million people — larger than Paris, larger than London, rivaled in Europe perhaps only by Constantinople. Its libraries held hundreds of thousands of manuscripts. Its scholars translated Aristotle, advanced algebra, refined astronomical tables, and debated philosophy. The Great Mosque of Córdoba, with its forest of striped arches, was an architectural marvel that still stops visitors in their tracks today.

Al-Andalus was never a simple monolith, however. It was ethnically layered — Arab aristocrats, Berber soldiers, converted Iberian Christians (called Mozarabs), Jews, and enslaved peoples from across the Mediterranean world all occupied distinct and sometimes volatile social positions. The caliphate's brilliance was real, but so were its tensions. When the great caliph Abd al-Rahman III died in 961 and the effective power passed to the strongman Al-Mansur, and then, after Al-Mansur's death in 1002, the caliphate splintered into the taifa kingdoms — small, quarrelsome successor states — the political geography of the peninsula shifted in ways that would prove fatal to Muslim political unity over the long run.

---

The Story of the Reconquista

The traditional starting point is the Battle of Covadonga, fought around 722, where Pelayo — a Visigothic nobleman of disputed origins — defeated a Muslim force in the Asturian mountains. Later chroniclers would inflate this skirmish into something mythic, the founding moment of Christian resistance. In reality, it was probably a small engagement that the southern governors barely registered. But mythology matters in history. Covadonga became the symbolic seed from which the Kingdom of Asturias, and eventually León, would grow.

For the first two centuries or so, the "reconquest" was less a crusade than a slow, opportunistic creep southward. Christian rulers raided, settled, and depopulated frontier zones, creating the vast empty buffer lands of the meseta — the high central plateau — that would later be repopulated by settlers granted special charters. The Duero River valley became a contested march, then gradually a Christian zone. The process was as much about colonization and agrarian settlement as it was about military conquest.

The fragmentation of the caliphate after 1002 changed everything. Suddenly the Christian kings of León, Castile, Navarre, and the nascent County of Barcelona found themselves dealing not with a unified, powerful state but with a dozen smaller ones, some of which were willing to pay tribute — the famous parias — to avoid being attacked. Alfonso VI of León and Castile, one of the most ambitious rulers of the eleventh century, extracted enormous sums from the taifa kings and then, in 1085, captured Toledo, the old Visigothic capital sitting at the heart of the peninsula. It was a psychological and strategic earthquake. Toledo had been under Muslim rule for nearly four centuries.

The taifa kings, desperate, invited in a new force from North Africa: the Almoravids, Berber reformers who had built an empire across the Maghreb. The Almoravids crossed the strait, defeated Alfonso VI at the Battle of Sagrajas in 1086, and temporarily halted the Christian advance. But they also absorbed the taifas into their empire, which created resentments of its own. The Almoravids were followed, in the twelfth century, by the Almohads — another North African Berber dynasty, even more religiously austere — who won the stunning victory of Alarcos against Alfonso VIII of Castile in 1195. For a moment, the momentum seemed to reverse.

It didn't. In 1212, at Las Navas de Tolosa, a coalition of Christian kings — Castile, Aragon, Navarre, Portugal — supported by crusaders from across Europe, shattered the Almohad army. Las Navas de Tolosa is one of those battles where historians genuinely argue about scale and significance, but the broad consensus is that it broke Almohad military power in Iberia decisively. Within a generation, the great southern cities fell in rapid succession: Córdoba in 1236, Valencia in 1238, Seville in 1248. By the mid-thirteenth century, the Muslim political presence in Iberia had contracted to the Emirate of Granada, a sophisticated but vulnerable kingdom tucked into the mountains of Andalusia.

Granada survived for another two and a half centuries through a combination of geography, diplomatic agility, and the periodic distraction of the Christian kingdoms by their own internal conflicts. It finally fell in 1492 — the same year Columbus sailed, a coincidence that is not entirely coincidental, since the same monarchs, Ferdinand and Isabella, were trying to project Iberian power outward in multiple directions simultaneously.

---

The Scholarly Debate: Was There a "Reconquista" at All?

Here is where the history gets genuinely interesting, and genuinely contested.

The word Reconquista was not widely used by medieval people themselves. It is largely a nineteenth-century construction, popularized by Spanish nationalist historians who wanted a grand narrative of Christian Spain's providential recovery of its homeland. For them, the story was linear, teleological, and essentially religious: a unified Christian people, separated from their land by Muslim invaders, slowly and heroically reclaiming what was rightfully theirs.

Modern historians have pushed back hard on almost every element of that framing. The Christian kingdoms were not unified — they fought each other constantly. The religious motivation, while real, was often secondary to dynastic ambition, economic interest, and political opportunism. Christian kings routinely allied with Muslim rulers against other Christian kings, and vice versa. The mercenary soldier Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar — El Cid — serves as a perfect emblem of this complexity: celebrated in later legend as the great Christian hero of the Reconquista, he spent significant portions of his career fighting for Muslim rulers. When he carved out his own principality in Valencia, it was less a crusading triumph than a personal empire built through pragmatic violence.

Scholars like Brian Catlos and Simon Barton have emphasized the degree to which medieval Iberia was a world of fluid, interest-driven alliances rather than a civilizational clash. Others, like Joseph O'Callaghan, have argued that crusading ideology was a genuine and powerful force, especially from the twelfth century onward, when the papacy explicitly granted crusade status to Iberian campaigns and warriors from across Europe joined in. Both things can be true. The Reconquista was simultaneously a religious project and a deeply cynical political one, depending on who you were, when you lived, and what you needed.

There is also the question of convivencia — the idea that medieval Iberia was a space of relative religious coexistence and cultural exchange. This concept, associated particularly with the historian Américo Castro, has become its own contested terrain. The cultural exchange was undeniably real: Christian scholars in Toledo translated Arabic texts into Latin, introducing classical philosophy back into European intellectual life. Jews served as physicians, translators, and administrators for both Muslim and Christian rulers. Architecture, poetry, music, and agriculture all showed deep cross-cultural borrowing. But "coexistence" should not be romanticized into tolerance in any modern sense. Persecution, forced conversion, and communal violence were also features of the landscape, and they intensified as the centuries wore on and Christian kingdoms consolidated their power.

---

The Aftermath: What Rose from the Ruins

The fall of Granada in 1492 did not end the story — it opened new and darker chapters. Within months, Ferdinand and Isabella issued the Alhambra Decree, expelling the Jews of Spain. Tens of thousands fled, scattering across North Africa, the Ottoman Empire, and the rest of Europe, carrying Iberian culture and memory with them in the form of the Ladino language still spoken by Sephardic communities today.

The Muslims of Granada were initially promised freedom of religion under the terms of surrender. Those promises eroded quickly. By 1502, Muslims in Castile were given the choice of conversion or exile. The converted Muslims — called Moriscos — were never fully trusted by Old Christian society, and in 1609, nearly a century after the conquest, they were expelled en masse. Roughly 300,000 people were driven out.

The institutional machinery developed during the Reconquista — the military orders, the frontier settlement practices, the ideology of holy war against infidels — did not simply dissolve in 1492. It was exported. The conquistadors who crossed the Atlantic to conquer the Americas in the decades after 1492 were shaped by the culture of the Reconquista: its mixture of religious zeal and naked material ambition, its practices of dividing conquered lands among settlers, its habit of demanding submission from peoples defined as outside Christendom. The Reconquista did not end in Granada. In a real sense, it crossed the ocean.

---

Why It Matters

The Reconquista is not a comfortable story, and that is precisely why it repays careful attention. It is a seven-century reminder that history rarely moves in straight lines, that the labels we attach to historical processes — "reconquest," "crusade," "coexistence" — are always simplifications that serve someone's purposes, and that the same events can look radically different depending on whose vantage point you occupy.

It raises questions that remain urgently alive: How do societies manage the presence of religious and ethnic minorities? What happens when the ideology of holy war becomes entangled with the machinery of the state? How do conquered peoples preserve identity across generations of political subordination? And how do the habits of mind formed in one theater of conflict — the frontier, the crusade, the forced conversion — travel and mutate when they are applied to new peoples in new places?

The Iberian Peninsula today — Spain and Portugal — is shaped in ways both visible and invisible by those seven and a half centuries of struggle and exchange. The architecture of Seville and Granada, the linguistic traces of Arabic in Spanish (alcázar, acequia, ojalá), the Sephardic communities that still maintain Ladino, the ongoing scholarly argument about what medieval Iberia actually was: these are not footnotes to the story. They are the story, still unfolding.

History, as the Reconquista teaches us, is never really over. It is only ever becoming something else.
