The Daily Brief

The Two Swords of Islam: How the Ottoman-Safavid Rivalry Carved the Modern Middle East

---

Early Modern Era | Middle East and Anatolia

---

The Hook

On the morning of August 23, 1514, two armies faced each other on a broad plain near the town of Chaldiran, in what is today northwestern Iran. One force fought beneath the red and gold banners of Shah Ismail I, the charismatic young ruler who had united Persia under a new and fiercely held religious identity. The other moved in disciplined ranks behind the crescent standard of Sultan Selim I — "Selim the Grim," as history would remember him — a man so ruthless that he had executed his own brothers and nephews to secure his throne. By nightfall, the field would be soaked in blood, Ismail's cavalry shattered by Ottoman cannon fire, and the shape of a civilization irrevocably altered. But the battle of Chaldiran was not simply a military contest between two empires. It was the opening act of a rivalry that would last more than two centuries, divide the Islamic world along lines of theology and politics, and leave borders and identities that the modern Middle East still lives inside.

---

The World Before

To understand what was at stake at Chaldiran, you have to imagine a Middle East that was still, in many respects, fluid. At the opening of the sixteenth century, the great Timurid order that had once stretched from Anatolia to Central Asia had collapsed. The Mamluk Sultanate still controlled Egypt and the Levant, but it was aging and brittle. Anatolia itself had only recently been consolidated under Ottoman rule after decades of wrestling with rival Turkish beyliks. The eastern Iranian plateau, Mesopotamia, and the Caucasus were a mosaic of competing tribes, small dynasties, and Sufi brotherhoods — zones where religious and political authority blurred together in ways that made them simultaneously volatile and susceptible to charismatic leadership.

Into this vacuum, two extraordinary institutions were rising simultaneously. The Ottoman Empire, founded in the late thirteenth century on the northwestern edge of the former Seljuk domains, had by 1500 already absorbed Constantinople, the Balkans, and most of Anatolia. It styled itself as the foremost Sunni power in the world — the defender of orthodox Islam, the guardian of the holy cities, the heir to the caliphate's prestige. Its administrative genius lay in its devshirme system, its professional janissary infantry, and a legal architecture that blended Islamic sharia with sultanic law codes called kanun.

But the Safavids were something altogether different and, to the Ottomans, deeply alarming. They emerged from a Sufi order — the Safaviyya — based in Ardabil in northwestern Iran. Originally Sunni, the order had gradually absorbed Shia devotional currents, millenarian expectations, and the fierce loyalty of Turkic-speaking nomadic warriors called the Qizilbash, meaning "redheads" for the distinctive crimson headgear they wore to honor the house of Ali. When the teenage Ismail swept out of Azerbaijan in 1501 and captured Tabriz, he did not merely found a dynasty. He declared Twelver Shia Islam the official religion of his new state and demanded conversion — sometimes at swordpoint — across the Iranian plateau. It was an act of breathtaking audacity. For the first time since the early Islamic centuries, a major polity was making Shia identity a matter of state policy.

---

The Story

The theological stakes could hardly have been higher. Twelver Shia Islam holds that the rightful leadership of the Muslim community passed through a line of twelve imams descended from the Prophet Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, Ali ibn Abi Talib. The twelfth imam, it teaches, went into occultation — a kind of divine hiding — in the ninth century and will return as a messianic figure at the end of time. Ismail himself was understood by his Qizilbash followers in almost divine terms, a semi-messianic figure whose authority derived from his descent from the imams. To Sunni Ottoman eyes, this looked not merely like heresy but like a revolutionary movement aimed at subverting the entire Sunni order of the world.

The friction was not only theological. The Safavid state sat astride key trade routes between the Mediterranean and Central Asia, and it actively courted the Qizilbash tribes inside Ottoman Anatolia — tribes who were already restive, already drawing on the same Shia-inflected Sufi currents that had energized Ismail's original movement. Selim I, who came to power in 1512 after deposing his own father Bayezid II, understood the Safavid challenge as an existential one. He reportedly ordered the massacre of tens of thousands of Shia-sympathizing Anatolians before he even crossed the border into Safavid territory — a brutal preemptive act that tells you everything about how the Ottomans perceived the threat from within.

The campaign of 1514 was thus a war for the soul of the Islamic world as much as for territory. Selim drove his army eastward through the brutal summer heat of Anatolia, stretching his supply lines to the breaking point. Ismail, following a scorched-earth strategy, refused pitched battle for as long as he could, hoping to exhaust the Ottomans in the harsh terrain of eastern Anatolia. But at Chaldiran, the confrontation became unavoidable. The outcome was decided in hours. The Safavid Qizilbash cavalry, legendary for its ferocity, charged headlong into Ottoman artillery and musket fire — weapons the Safavids either lacked or disdained as unworthy of true warriors. The slaughter was immense. Ismail himself barely escaped. The Ottomans occupied Tabriz briefly before withdrawing, unable to hold so distant a conquest with their supply lines exhausted.

Chaldiran did not destroy the Safavid state, and here is the crucial irony: it may have saved it. The defeat shattered the quasi-divine aura around Ismail, forcing the Safavids to evolve. They could no longer rely on messianic charisma alone. They had to build real institutions — a standing army, a bureaucracy, a legal establishment. Over the following decades, under Ismail's son Tahmasp I and later the brilliant Shah Abbas I (r. 1588–1629), the Safavid state matured into one of the most sophisticated empires of the early modern world, with its capital at Isfahan, its workshops producing some of the finest carpets, miniatures, and tilework in human history, and its Shia clerical establishment gradually gaining the institutional depth that would echo, centuries later, in the Islamic Revolution of 1979.

The wars between the two empires did not end at Chaldiran. They erupted again and again — in 1532, in 1548, in 1578, and through the catastrophic Ottoman-Safavid War of 1603–1618, when Shah Abbas temporarily recaptured Tabriz, Baghdad, and much of the Caucasus. The Treaty of Zuhab in 1639 finally established a frontier between the two empires that was, with remarkable fidelity, the precursor to the modern border between Turkey and Iran. Mesopotamia — the land between the Tigris and Euphrates, the ancient heart of civilization — fell definitively into Ottoman hands, setting the stage for what would eventually become Iraq.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Historians have argued for generations about what this rivalry was really about. Was it primarily a religious war — a Sunni-Shia conflict that hardened sectarian identities that had previously been more fluid? Or was it fundamentally a geopolitical contest between two expansionist states that used religion as a mobilizing ideology? The answer, most serious scholars now believe, is both, and the interaction between the two dimensions is what makes the rivalry so historically potent.

The work of scholars like Rudi Matthee on Safavid Iran and Cemal Kafadar on Ottoman identity has complicated older narratives that treated the Shia-Sunni divide as a timeless, primordial feature of the Islamic world. The evidence suggests that before the Safavid revolution, religious identity in the Middle East was far more syncretic and layered than it would become afterward. The Safavid project of converting Iran to Twelver Shia Islam — importing Arab Shia scholars from Lebanon and Iraq to build a clerical infrastructure, suppressing Sunni and Sufi practices, rewriting the religious landscape of the entire plateau — was a deliberate act of state formation. The Ottoman insistence on Sunni orthodoxy hardened in direct response. The two empires, in effect, defined each other.

There is also genuine uncertainty about the human costs of this process. How many Anatolian Alevis and Iranian Sunnis were killed, displaced, or forcibly converted during these centuries? The sources are incomplete, the numbers disputed. What is clear is that by the time the rivalry wound down in the mid-seventeenth century, the religious geography of the Middle East had been fundamentally reorganized. Iran was Shia. The Arab lands and Anatolia were Sunni. The border between them was not merely political but confessional.

---

The Aftermath

The consequences radiated outward in every direction. The Ottoman absorption of the Arab lands — Egypt, Syria, the Hijaz, Mesopotamia — following Selim's victory over the Mamluks in 1517 was partly enabled by the eastern preoccupation with the Safavid threat. The Ottomans became the custodians of Mecca and Medina, cementing their claim to Sunni leadership but also binding them to the Arab world in ways that would shape the empire's character for centuries. Meanwhile, Safavid Iran developed a distinctly Persian-inflected Shia civilization, one that increasingly distinguished itself from the Arab and Turkish worlds through language, art, and religious practice.

Shah Abbas I's Isfahan became one of the great cities of the seventeenth century, its central Naqsh-e Jahan square one of the largest public spaces in the world, its bazaars trading with Venice, the Mughal court, and the Portuguese Estado da India simultaneously. European powers, recognizing the strategic value of an enemy on the Ottoman eastern flank, sent diplomats and military advisers to Isfahan. The Safavid court received them with sophisticated interest. This triangular diplomacy — Ottomans, Safavids, and European powers maneuvering against each other — was one of the first great exercises in what we might now call balance-of-power politics.

When the Safavid dynasty collapsed in 1722 under Afghan invasion, the power vacuum it left was eventually filled by the Qajar dynasty, which maintained the Shia identity of the Iranian state. The clerical establishment the Safavids had built proved more durable than the dynasty itself. It survived, deepened its roots, and eventually — in 1979 — produced the Islamic Republic of Iran, in which Shia clerics hold formal state power for the first time in history. The line from Ismail's declaration in Tabriz in 1501 to Khomeini's revolution nearly five centuries later is not straight, and historians rightly resist simple teleology, but it is traceable.

---

Why It Matters

The Ottoman-Safavid rivalry matters today for reasons that go beyond the academic. The Sunni-Shia divide that shapes so much of contemporary Middle Eastern politics — the proxy conflicts in Yemen, Syria, Lebanon, and Iraq; the rivalry between Saudi Arabia and Iran; the identity struggles of Shia communities from Bahrain to Pakistan — did not emerge from the seventh century in the form we see it now. It was substantially constructed, hardened, and institutionalized by two early modern empires competing for power, legitimacy, and survival. Understanding this does not make the divisions less real, but it does make them less inevitable. They were made by human choices, by political ambition, by the contingencies of battle.

The story also illuminates something about how borders are made. The frontier between Turkey and Iran, ratified in 1639 by the Treaty of Zuhab, has proven more stable than almost any other boundary in the modern Middle East — more stable than the Sykes-Picot lines drawn by European powers in 1916, more stable than the borders of states assembled and dissolved in the twentieth century. It endures because it corresponds to something real: a confessional and cultural divide that two empires spent 150 years carving into the landscape of civilization. The modern world did not invent the Middle East's divisions. It inherited them, often without knowing where they came from.

And there is something else, something harder to categorize. The art, architecture, and poetry that flourished in the courts of both empires during these same centuries of conflict — the Ottoman classical tradition, the Safavid miniature, the great poets of both Persian and Turkish — remind us that civilizations do not only destroy. They also create, often most brilliantly under pressure, in the space between threat and ambition.

---
