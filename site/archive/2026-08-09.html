The Bandung Conference and the Non-Aligned World

April 18–24, 1955 · Bandung, Indonesia

---

The photograph is easy to overlook if you don't know what you're seeing. Twenty-nine men in suits, nehru jackets, and military dress stand together on the steps of a colonial-era building in a mid-sized Indonesian city, squinting into the tropical sun. Most of them will be dead within two decades, some violently. Their countries, collectively, contain more than half the world's population. And for one week in April 1955, they are attempting something that had never quite been tried before: they are meeting not as the clients of great powers, not as colonies or protectorates or spheres of influence, but as sovereign nations in their own right, with their own agenda, their own grievances, and their own vision for what the postwar world ought to become.

The Bandung Conference — formally the Asian-African Conference — lasted six days. It produced a communiqué, a set of principles, and an atmosphere. The communiqué is largely forgotten. The principles were frequently violated by the very men who endorsed them. But the atmosphere — the electric, improbable sense that the formerly colonized world could speak in a single voice — echoed for decades. It still does.

---

The World Before: Two Blocs and a Billion Left Over

To understand what Bandung meant, you have to feel the suffocating geometry of the early Cold War. By 1955, the world had been sorted, or was being sorted, into two camps. On one side stood the United States and its NATO allies, champions of liberal capitalism, guardians of a "free world" whose freedom had notable exceptions. On the other stood the Soviet Union and its satellite states, advocates of communist revolution, patrons of liberation movements wherever such patronage served Moscow's interests. Both superpowers had nuclear weapons. Both were spending fortunes on military alliances, propaganda, and covert operations. Both were absolutely convinced that the future of humanity depended on their side winning.

And then there was everyone else.

The "everyone else" was enormous — most of Africa, most of Asia, the Middle East, much of Latin America. These were places that had spent the previous century or two under European domination, places where the word "independence" still tasted new, where the borders on the map had often been drawn by strangers in London or Paris or Brussels with very little concern for the people who lived there. The Korean War had just ended in a bloody stalemate. France was losing its grip on Indochina and Algeria. The Dutch had only reluctantly surrendered the East Indies — which became Indonesia — in 1949. The British Empire was fraying at every seam.

For the leaders of these newly independent or nearly independent nations, the Cold War presented a particular kind of trap. Both superpowers wanted allies, clients, bases, and raw materials. Both offered aid — with strings. To sign with Washington meant accepting American anticommunism as your own, often including American support for the very colonial powers that had oppressed you. To sign with Moscow meant accepting Soviet tutelage, ideological conformity, and the suspicion of your own population. Neither option was especially dignified. Neither addressed the actual problems — poverty, illiteracy, underdevelopment, the lingering economic structures of colonialism — that most of these nations faced.

A few leaders had begun to ask, quietly at first and then with increasing confidence: what if you simply refused to choose?

---

The Gathering: Colombo, Bogor, and the Road to Bandung

The conference did not emerge from nowhere. Its immediate parents were the "Colombo Powers" — a group of five Asian prime ministers who had met in Sri Lanka (then Ceylon) in April 1954: Jawaharlal Nehru of India, Ali Sastroamidjojo of Indonesia, Mohammed Ali of Pakistan, Sir John Kotelawala of Ceylon, and U Nu of Burma. They were an unlikely coalition, divided by religion, political temperament, and territorial disputes, but united by a shared desire to be heard on the world stage without having to speak through a superpower's microphone.

They agreed to convene a larger meeting. In December 1954, they gathered again in Bogor, Indonesia, and hammered out the invitation list. Twenty-nine nations would be asked. The guest list itself was a political act: it included China (the People's Republic, not Taiwan), which infuriated Washington. It excluded the Soviet Union, which was, after all, a European power. It included nations with communist governments, neutral governments, and openly pro-Western governments — a deliberate mixing that reflected the organizers' insistence that this was not an anti-Western conference, merely a non-Western one.

When the delegates arrived in Bandung in April 1955, they represented nations containing roughly 1.5 billion people — about 54 percent of humanity. The city itself was decorated with banners and flowers. Indonesian schoolchildren lined the roads. There was a sense, recalled by many participants in memoirs written years later, of genuine historical electricity.

---

The Conference Itself: Five Days of Argument and Theater

The sessions were held at the Gedung Merdeka — Freedom Hall, a building the Dutch had constructed for their own colonial gatherings, now repurposed with pointed symbolism. The proceedings were simultaneously a diplomatic conference, a political theater, and a genuine argument among people who agreed on very little except their right to be there.

Nehru was the towering intellectual presence, lean and imperious, the philosopher-king of decolonization. He had been developing his concept of panchsheel — five principles of peaceful coexistence, including mutual non-interference in internal affairs and respect for sovereignty — and he used Bandung as a platform to propagate them. He was also, characteristically, somewhat condescending toward leaders he considered less sophisticated, which did not endear him to everyone.

Zhou Enlai, the Chinese premier, was arguably the conference's greatest diplomatic success story. He arrived in Bandung under a cloud: many of the attending nations were deeply suspicious of Chinese intentions in Southeast Asia, and several were actively frightened of Chinese support for communist insurgencies in their own countries. Zhou proceeded to charm almost everyone. He was urbane, self-deprecating, precise. He emphasized coexistence over revolution. He offered bilateral negotiations to nervous neighbors. He transformed what could have been a conference that isolated China into one that, at least temporarily, brought it into the fellowship of the non-aligned world. (It is worth noting that Zhou's charm offensive was also a masterwork of strategic misdirection — Chinese support for regional communist movements did not actually stop — but at Bandung, the performance was flawless.)

The conference's most dramatic moment came from an unexpected direction. Sir John Kotelawala of Ceylon, the most pro-Western of the Colombo Powers, delivered a speech denouncing Soviet imperialism in Eastern Europe in terms just as sharp as any denunciation of Western colonialism. The room went tense. Nehru, who had carefully avoided any language that might embarrass the Soviets, was furious. The Iraqi and Pakistani delegations applauded. The debate that followed — about whether communist domination constituted colonialism — was one of the most honest arguments of the entire Cold War, conducted without either superpower in the room.

The final communiqué endorsed what became known as the "Ten Principles of Bandung": respect for sovereignty, non-aggression, non-interference, equality, peaceful coexistence. These were not new ideas. What was new was who was saying them, and to whom.

---

The Scholarly Debate: What Did Bandung Actually Accomplish?

Historians have argued about Bandung's significance ever since, and the argument has never quite resolved itself.

The skeptics have strong material. The non-aligned movement that grew from Bandung was never truly non-aligned: Nehru's India accepted Soviet military equipment; Nasser's Egypt played both sides with extraordinary dexterity; Sukarno's Indonesia slid toward the PKI, the Indonesian Communist Party, before the catastrophic coup and counter-coup of 1965 that killed perhaps half a million people and brought the firmly pro-American Suharto to power. The "Bandung Spirit" was repeatedly invoked by leaders who were simultaneously building personality cults, imprisoning political opponents, and soliciting aid from whichever superpower offered better terms that week. The gap between the conference's rhetoric and the subsequent behavior of its participants was, in many cases, vast.

There is also a sharp feminist critique of Bandung that has grown louder in recent decades: the conference was overwhelmingly male, its vision of national liberation was often explicitly patriarchal, and the "peoples" whose solidarity was proclaimed were frequently represented by governments that had no interest in women's political participation. The nations of the Global South were speaking — but not everyone in those nations was speaking.

And yet the maximalist dismissal feels equally unsatisfying. Bandung mattered as a moment of self-conception. It was the first time leaders from across the formerly colonized world had gathered on their own terms, without European or American chairs at the head of the table, and declared themselves historical actors rather than historical objects. The intellectual lineage that runs from Bandung through the Non-Aligned Movement (formally established in Belgrade in 1961) through the New International Economic Order debates of the 1970s is real, even if the practical achievements were modest. The conference gave a name and a grammar to a political aspiration that had been building for decades.

The scholar Vijay Prashad, whose work has done much to revive serious academic interest in Bandung, calls the project it represented "the Third World project" — an attempt to build a different kind of internationalism, rooted in solidarity among the formerly colonized rather than in the logic of Cold War blocs. That project largely failed, Prashad acknowledges, but the failure was not inevitable, and understanding why it failed tells us something important about the structure of global power.

---

The Aftermath: A Movement That Outlived Its Moment

The Non-Aligned Movement held its first formal summit in Belgrade in 1961, with Tito of Yugoslavia, Nehru, Nasser, Sukarno, and Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana as its presiding spirits. It was a remarkable gathering of remarkable egos. It also marked the beginning of the movement's institutionalization — and, in some ways, its dilution. As more nations joined, the definition of "non-aligned" grew elastic enough to accommodate almost anyone. Cuba joined while hosting Soviet military advisors. By the 1970s, the movement had more than a hundred members and a coherent program of demands — for debt relief, fair commodity prices, technology transfer, reform of international financial institutions — that constituted the New International Economic Order. The NIEO was debated seriously for roughly a decade before the debt crisis of the 1980s, structural adjustment programs, and the overwhelming dominance of neoliberal economics effectively ended the conversation.

Bandung's intellectual legacy proved more durable than its political legacy. The conference inspired a generation of writers, artists, and intellectuals across the Global South — from Frantz Fanon, whose The Wretched of the Earth is in some ways Bandung's philosophical companion text, to the poets of the Négritude movement, to the architects of African socialism. The idea that the formerly colonized had not only political interests but a distinct cultural and intellectual perspective worth contributing to world civilization — that idea drew real sustenance from what happened in that hall in West Java.

And the conference itself has experienced a remarkable scholarly and political revival in the twenty-first century. As China has risen, as the BRICS grouping has grown, as the phrase "Global South" has returned to diplomatic vocabulary after decades of relative dormancy, Bandung has been repeatedly cited as a founding moment, a usable past. Whether this invocation is historically accurate or politically convenient — or both — is itself a question worth asking.

---

Why It Matters

The Bandung Conference is easy to sentimentalize and easy to dismiss, and both responses miss the point. What the conference captured — imperfectly, temporarily, but genuinely — was the problem that has never gone away: how do nations with less power navigate a world designed by and for nations with more of it?

The specific Cold War architecture that gave Bandung its urgency is gone. But the underlying structural question is not. When countries in the Global South today refuse to take clear sides in the conflict between the United States and China, when they demand that climate finance be treated as a matter of historical justice rather than charity, when they insist that the rules of international trade and intellectual property be renegotiated rather than simply accepted — they are, whether they know it or not, working in a tradition that has Bandung somewhere in its ancestry.

The men on those steps in 1955 did not build the world they wanted. Very few political actors ever do. But they named something real: the aspiration of the majority of humanity to participate in shaping global order rather than merely enduring it. That aspiration has proven more durable than any of the specific institutions or agreements it generated. It keeps returning, in new forms, wearing new names, because the conditions that produced it have never fully changed.

The photograph, looked at long enough, stops being a curiosity and becomes a mirror.
