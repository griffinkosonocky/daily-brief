The Daily Brief

The Field That Made an Empire: Babur at Panipat, 1526

---

April 21, 1526. A flat, dusty plain north of Delhi. An outnumbered invader from Central Asia looks across at one of the largest armies in Asia. What happens in the next few hours will reshape the subcontinent for three centuries.

---

The Hook

The night before the battle, Zahir ud-Din Muhammad Babur could not sleep. This was not unusual — he rarely could before a fight — but the stakes this time were different from anything he had faced in a life already crowded with desperate gambles. He was forty-three years old, twice exiled from his ancestral homeland of Samarkand, a king who had lost more kingdoms than most men ever rule. He had crossed the Khyber Pass four times in the previous decade, raiding, probing, searching for a foothold on the subcontinent that lay shimmering and vast to the south. Now, finally, he had committed. There would be no raid this time, no orderly retreat to Kabul with plunder and prisoners. He had brought his whole force — perhaps twelve thousand fighting men, some accounts say fifteen — and he had placed them on a plain near a small market town called Panipat, about fifty miles north of Delhi.

Across that plain, Ibrahim Lodi, Sultan of Delhi, was massing an army that contemporary accounts numbered anywhere from one hundred thousand to one million men. Even accounting for the medieval habit of inflating enemy numbers to make victory seem more glorious, Ibrahim's force was enormous — perhaps eighty thousand soldiers and, more terrifyingly, somewhere between one hundred and one thousand war elephants. The earth shook when they moved. Babur, writing later in his extraordinary memoir the Baburnama, described the anxiety in his camp with characteristic directness. His men were frightened. Some wanted to go home.

He gave a speech. Then he waited for dawn.

---

The World Before

To understand what Panipat meant, you have to understand the world it interrupted.

The Delhi Sultanate, by 1526, was a dynasty in its terminal convulsions. For more than three centuries, a succession of Turkic and Afghan dynasties had ruled northern India from the red sandstone walls of Delhi — the Mamluks, the Khaljis, the Tughlaqs, the Sayyids, and finally the Lodis, an Afghan clan that had seized power in 1451. At its height, the Sultanate had been a formidable force, repelling Mongol invasions, extending its reach deep into the Deccan, and presiding over a cosmopolitan court where Persian was the language of culture and power. But by the early sixteenth century, the structure was cracking. The Afghan nobility was fractious and resentful of Ibrahim Lodi's autocratic temperament. Regional governors were peeling away. The Rajput kingdoms to the west and south were reasserting themselves under the confederation of Rana Sanga of Mewar, a battle-scarred warrior king who had lost an eye, an arm, and a leg to various conflicts and somehow kept fighting.

India in 1526 was not a unified entity waiting to be conquered. It was a mosaic of competing powers — the Lodi Sultanate, Rajput confederacies, the Sultanates of Bengal, Malwa, and Gujarat, the Deccan Sultanates, and countless smaller principalities. There was no single "India" to fall; there was only a shifting balance of power that a sufficiently decisive blow might permanently rearrange.

Babur himself came from a world equally turbulent. He was a Timurid prince, a direct descendant of both Timur (Tamerlane) on his father's side and Genghis Khan on his mother's — a lineage so ferocious it reads like a genealogy of catastrophe. Born in 1483 in the Fergana Valley in what is now Uzbekistan, he had inherited a small kingdom at eleven years old and spent his adolescence in a brutal scramble for Samarkand, the jewel of the Timurid world. He took it twice. He lost it twice, driven out by the Uzbek confederation of the Shaybanids. By his mid-twenties, Babur had been reduced to a fugitive in the mountains of Afghanistan, surviving on mulberries and loyalty he could barely afford to repay.

He captured Kabul in 1504 and made it his base. But Kabul, for a Timurid prince with imperial blood and imperial ambitions, was a consolation prize. The wealth he needed to pay his soldiers and reward his nobles had to come from somewhere. He looked south, toward Hindustan, toward Delhi.

---

The Story of the Battle

Babur arrayed his forces at Panipat with a tactical intelligence that belied the apparent hopelessness of his position. He had spent months preparing. His left flank rested on the town of Panipat itself, his right on a ditch his men had spent days digging and filling with felled trees. The center was protected by a line of seven hundred carts, chained together, with gaps between them through which his matchlock gunners and artillery could fire. This arrangement — the araba, or wagon fort — was borrowed from Ottoman practice, and it was relatively new to the subcontinent. Babur had also acquired Ottoman gunners, including a master of artillery named Ustad Ali Quli, and field cannon that Ibrahim Lodi's army had never encountered in battle.

The Lodi force, when it finally advanced on April 21, found itself funneled. Ibrahim's commanders, seeing the flanks blocked, pushed their enormous army toward the center — and toward the guns. The matchlocks and cannon opened fire. The elephants, unaccustomed to the noise and smoke, panicked. They wheeled and trampled their own men. The Lodi cavalry, compressed by the terrain and the chaos, could not maneuver. Meanwhile Babur's flanking cavalry — his tulughma, a Mongol encircling tactic he had perfected over years of warfare — swung wide around both ends and fell on the Lodi rear.

Ibrahim Lodi died on the field. His body was later found among thousands of others, identified by his royal garments. He had, by all accounts, fought personally and bravely. It did not matter. The battle lasted perhaps three hours. The Delhi Sultanate, which had ruled northern India for more than three centuries, ceased to exist before noon.

Babur rode to Delhi. Then he rode to Agra, where Ibrahim's treasury waited — a treasure so vast that each of his nobles received a share so large that Babur recorded, with evident satisfaction, that the word "lakh" (one hundred thousand) entered common usage in his camp that week as a unit for counting coins.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Historians have argued for decades about what, exactly, made the difference at Panipat. The traditional answer — and the one Babur himself promoted — is gunpowder. His artillery and matchlocks gave him a decisive technological edge over an army that had not adapted to the new weapons of the early modern world. This interpretation has the appealing clarity of a turning-point narrative: the medieval meets the modern and loses.

But revisionist historians have complicated this picture considerably. Jos Gommans and others have pointed out that firearms were not entirely unknown in northern India by 1526; the Lodis had some exposure to them. The decisive factor, these scholars argue, was not technology per se but tactical integration — Babur's ability to combine artillery, infantry, and fast-moving cavalry in a coordinated system that Ibrahim's commanders simply could not counter. The tulughma flanking movement, in this reading, matters as much as the guns.

Others have emphasized the political dimension. Ibrahim Lodi's own nobles were lukewarm at best. Some, including Daulat Khan Lodi, the governor of Punjab, had actually invited Babur south in the hope of using him as a weapon against the sultan — a classic miscalculation by a regional power that thought it could control an outside force. Rana Sanga of Mewar, meanwhile, was watching from the west, expecting Babur to take his plunder and leave, as previous raiders had always done. The possibility that Babur intended to stay seems not to have been fully credited.

And then there is the question of Ibrahim himself. Was he simply a bad general, or was he commanding an army so politically divided that no general could have held it together? The Baburnama, our richest source for the battle, is also an autobiography — Babur had every reason to make his victory seem more impressive and his opponent more formidable. The chronicles on the Lodi side are fragmentary and partial. We are, in the end, reading the victor's account of his own genius.

---

The Aftermath

Babur did not leave. This was the fact that changed everything.

He spent the remaining four years of his life — he died in 1530, at forty-seven, worn out by a lifetime of campaigning — consolidating a new empire. He defeated Rana Sanga at the Battle of Khanwa in 1527, a harder fight than Panipat that required Babur to invoke jihad to stiffen his troops' resolve. He pushed east, defeating the Afghans of Bihar and Bengal at the Battle of Ghaghra in 1529. By his death, he controlled the Gangetic plain from Kabul to Bengal — the skeleton of what would become the Mughal Empire.

The empire his descendants built was extraordinary. His son Humayun nearly lost it all, driven into exile in Persia by the Afghan chieftain Sher Shah Suri in 1540. But Humayun returned, recaptured Delhi, and left the throne to a son born in the desert — Akbar, who would prove to be one of the most remarkable rulers in world history. Under Akbar (r. 1556–1605), the Mughal Empire became a genuine synthesis: Persian administrative sophistication, Central Asian military power, and Indian cultural depth fused into something new. Akbar abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims, patronized Hindu and Jain scholars alongside Muslim theologians, and created a court culture of dazzling cosmopolitan complexity.

The empire Babur founded at Panipat eventually encompassed most of the subcontinent. At its peak under Aurangzeb in the late seventeenth century, it was one of the largest and wealthiest empires on earth, home to perhaps one hundred fifty million people and generating more economic output than all of Western Europe combined. The Taj Mahal, built by Babur's great-great-grandson Shah Jahan as a tomb for his wife, is perhaps the most recognizable building in the world — and it would not exist without the outcome of a three-hour battle on a dusty plain in 1526.

The Mughal legacy is also the contested legacy of modern South Asia. The architectural heritage — the Red Fort, Fatehpur Sikri, the Lahore Mosque — is embedded in the landscapes of India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh. The administrative traditions, the Persian-inflected Urdu language, the syncretic cultural forms that blended Hindu and Islamic artistic vocabularies — all of this flowed from the civilization Babur's victory made possible. And the tensions that eventually tore the empire apart, the conflicts between its plural religious communities, the resentments accumulated under later, less tolerant rulers, echo in the politics of the subcontinent to this day.

---

Why It Matters

Panipat is a story about what happens when a small, mobile, technologically adaptive force meets a larger but less flexible one — and about how the outcome of a single afternoon can redirect centuries of history. But it is also a story about something less dramatic and more durable: the question of what makes an empire legitimate in the eyes of those it rules.

Babur won at Panipat by superior tactics. But the Mughal Empire lasted as long as it did — and achieved what it achieved — because his descendants, at their best, understood that conquest is only the beginning. Akbar's genius was not military but political: the recognition that ruling a vast, diverse, religiously plural society required accommodation rather than domination, synthesis rather than suppression. That insight, and its repeated failure under rulers who abandoned it, is a lesson that has not lost its relevance.

Every generation produces leaders who believe that sufficient force can substitute for legitimacy. Panipat shows us what force alone can accomplish — a brilliant, decisive, world-changing victory. The Baburnama shows us something else: a man reflective enough to write honestly about his fears, his failures, and his loves, who planted melons from Kabul in the Indian soil because he missed the taste of home. Empires are made by armies. They are sustained, or not, by something harder to measure.

The plain at Panipat has hosted three battles that each reshaped South Asian history — 1526, 1556, and 1761. The ground there has a way of demanding that history pay attention. It still does.
