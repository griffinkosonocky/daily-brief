The Mountain of the Gods: How the Khmer Built Angkor Wat

The Daily Brief — Historical Read

---

A Stone That Remembers

Imagine standing in the jungle of what is now northwestern Cambodia, somewhere in the early twelfth century. The monsoon has just broken, and the air smells of wet earth and distant smoke. Through the trees, half a mile away, something impossible is rising above the canopy — five stone towers shaped like lotus buds, their sandstone faces catching the morning light and throwing it back at you in warm, reddish gold. Around those towers, a temple complex stretches across more than 400 acres. Workers are everywhere: quarrymen, sculptors, engineers, priests, and tens of thousands of laborers hauling stone on bamboo sledges through the mud. You are watching, in real time, the construction of the largest religious monument ever built by human hands.

Angkor Wat was not a monument that announced itself gradually to history. It erupted — commissioned, constructed, and substantially completed within a single reign, in roughly thirty-seven years. That fact alone is enough to stop a historian cold. Notre-Dame de Paris took nearly two centuries. The great cathedrals of medieval Europe were multigenerational projects, their builders dying before the spires were finished. Angkor Wat was done, more or less, in a lifetime. The question of how — and why — is one of the most compelling engineering and cultural mysteries of the medieval world.

---

The World Before the Stones Were Laid

To understand Angkor Wat, you have to understand the civilization that made it possible, because the temple did not appear from nowhere. It was the culmination of four centuries of Khmer ambition.

The Khmer Empire had been building itself since roughly 802 CE, when a king named Jayavarman II performed a ritual on Phnom Kulen, a sandstone plateau north of the great lake called Tonle Sap, and declared himself a devaraja — a god-king. The concept was partly borrowed from Hindu cosmology, partly an indigenous Khmer elaboration, and entirely political. By claiming to be the earthly manifestation of Shiva, Jayavarman II fused religious authority with royal power in a way that would shape Khmer culture for centuries. Every king who followed him inherited this divine responsibility: to build a temple that would serve as his spiritual center, his connection to the cosmic mountain of Hindu myth, and eventually his tomb.

The Khmer capital at Angkor, in what is now Siem Reap province, became one of the most sophisticated urban environments on the medieval planet. At its height, somewhere between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, greater Angkor may have supported a population of 750,000 to one million people — making it, by some estimates, the largest pre-industrial city in human history. The engine of this population was water. Angkor sat in a landscape defined by seasonal flooding from the Tonle Sap, which swells dramatically each monsoon season when the Mekong River reverses the flow of the Tonle Sap River. The Khmer did not simply adapt to this hydraulic reality. They mastered it.

They built an intricate network of reservoirs called barays, canals, and moats that captured monsoon floodwater and released it during the dry season for rice cultivation. This hydraulic system allowed Angkor's farmers to harvest multiple rice crops per year, generating the agricultural surplus that fed a massive urban and labor population. Without that surplus, there is no Angkor Wat. The temple was, in a very literal sense, built on rice.

---

The Reign of Suryavarman II and the Decision to Build

In 1113 CE, a prince named Suryavarman II seized the Khmer throne after a period of dynastic instability. Ancient inscriptions describe him leaping onto a rival king's war elephant and killing him — an image of decisive, almost reckless courage that seems consistent with what followed. He was a king who thought in grand scales.

Suryavarman II was a devotee of Vishnu, which was itself a departure — most of his predecessors had favored Shiva. And it was to Vishnu that he decided to dedicate his state temple, his devaraja monument, his bid for divine immortality. The site he chose was just south of the royal capital of Angkor Thom, on a flat plain bordered by the great moat that would become Angkor Wat's defining geographic feature.

Construction appears to have begun around 1116 CE and continued until approximately 1150 CE, the year Suryavarman II died. The scale of the undertaking is almost incomprehensible even now. The outer enclosure wall runs 3.6 kilometers in total length. The moat surrounding the complex is 190 meters wide. The central tower rises to 65 meters above the ground — roughly the height of a modern twenty-story building. And throughout the galleries of the temple, nearly half a kilometer of bas-relief carvings depict scenes from Hindu cosmology, military campaigns, and the daily life of the Khmer court in extraordinary detail.

The stone itself tells part of the story. Angkor Wat is built primarily from sandstone quarried at Phnom Kulen, the same sacred plateau where Jayavarman II had declared himself god-king three centuries earlier. The quarry is roughly 40 kilometers from the temple site. Scholars have estimated that somewhere between 5 and 10 million blocks of sandstone were transported to Angkor — some blocks weighing up to 1,500 kilograms. The logistics of moving that material, without wheeled vehicles capable of carrying such loads, without cranes in the modern sense, without steel tools, represent an organizational achievement as impressive as the architecture itself.

---

The Engineering Problem and the Khmer Solution

Here is where the story becomes genuinely mysterious, because we do not have a Khmer engineering manual. No detailed contemporary account of construction methods survives. What we have instead is the temple itself, and the careful inferences of modern archaeologists, engineers, and hydrologists who have spent decades trying to reverse-engineer what the Khmer did.

The sandstone blocks were almost certainly floated on rafts along a network of canals from Phnom Kulen to the construction site. The Khmer's mastery of hydraulic engineering — the same expertise that built the barays — would have made this feasible. Once at the site, the blocks were likely moved on sledges and rollers, hauled by teams of workers and possibly elephants. Ramps of earth and sand may have been used to raise stones to higher levels, the ramps growing as the towers grew, then demolished when construction was complete.

What makes Angkor Wat structurally remarkable is not just its size but its precision. The blocks are fitted together without mortar, relying instead on exact cutting and gravity to hold them in place — a technique similar to the dry-stone construction of the Inca, half a world away and two centuries later. The joints are so tight that in many places a sheet of paper cannot be inserted between blocks that have sat in the Cambodian jungle for nine hundred years. The alignment of the complex with astronomical phenomena — the sun rises directly over the central tower at the spring equinox when viewed from the main entrance — suggests that Khmer architects possessed sophisticated knowledge of solar geometry.

A 2007 study using satellite imagery and ground surveys, led by researchers at the University of Sydney's Greater Angkor Project, revealed that the hydraulic infrastructure supporting Angkor was even more elaborate than previously understood — a web of canals, embankments, and water-distribution channels covering hundreds of square kilometers. The same engineering culture that designed that infrastructure designed Angkor Wat. The temple and the city were not separate achievements. They were expressions of the same integrated intelligence.

---

The Scholarly Debate: Who Built It, and How Many?

The honest answer to "how many people built Angkor Wat?" is that we do not know. Estimates range from 50,000 to 300,000 workers, and the methodology behind every estimate involves significant assumptions. Some scholars argue the labor force was primarily composed of slaves and war captives. Others emphasize the role of semi-voluntary corvée labor — the Khmer system in which subjects owed the king a portion of their labor as a form of taxation. Still others suggest that religious motivation was a powerful driver: building the god-king's temple was both a civic duty and a spiritual act, and the workers may have understood themselves as participants in something sacred.

The inscriptions at Angkor are informative but not comprehensive. They tell us about kings and priests and battles, not about the daily lives of quarrymen and sculptors. Archaeological work has begun to fill some of these gaps — the Greater Angkor Project and subsequent research have identified worker settlements, food preparation areas, and evidence of the logistical networks that supported construction — but large questions remain.

There is also an ongoing debate about the original purpose of Angkor Wat. For most of its history, Western scholars assumed it was built as a temple and later converted into a funerary monument, since Suryavarman II's ashes (or some representation of them) appear to have been interred there. But more recent scholarship, drawing on the temple's unusual west-facing orientation — most Khmer temples face east, toward the rising sun; west is the direction of death and the ancestors in Hindu cosmology — suggests that Suryavarman II may have designed it as a funerary monument from the beginning. The astronomical alignments, including dramatic effects during the winter solstice as well as the equinoxes, add layers of intentionality that scholars are still unpacking.

---

After Suryavarman II: The Temple's Long Life

Suryavarman II died around 1150 CE, possibly on a military campaign against the Cham people of what is now Vietnam. The empire he left behind was powerful but not invulnerable. In 1177, the Cham retaliated with a naval assault that sacked Angkor itself — an event so traumatic that it appears in the bas-reliefs of the later temple of Bayon, carved under the king who would drive the Cham out and rebuild Khmer glory.

That king was Jayavarman VII, who came to power around 1181 CE and became, arguably, the most ambitious builder in Khmer history. He constructed the walled city of Angkor Thom, the face-tower temples of Bayon and Ta Prohm, and dozens of hospitals and rest houses across the empire. Under Jayavarman VII, the Khmer Empire also converted — at the royal level, at least — from Hinduism to Mahayana Buddhism. Angkor Wat itself was partially rededicated to Buddhist purposes during this period, and Buddhist iconography was added alongside the existing Hindu imagery.

The temple never entirely stopped being used. When Theravada Buddhism became dominant in Southeast Asia during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, Angkor Wat transitioned again, becoming a Theravada Buddhist monastery — a function it retains to this day, making it one of the oldest continuously operating religious sites in the world. Monks have lived within its walls, in various configurations, for the better part of nine centuries.

The city of Angkor itself was gradually abandoned as the Khmer capital after the fifteenth century, likely due to a combination of factors: the shifting political threat from the Thai kingdoms to the west, ecological stress on the hydraulic system, and possibly climate disruptions documented in tree-ring records that suggest prolonged droughts and severe monsoons alternating in ways that overwhelmed the water management infrastructure. The jungle moved in. But the temple stood.

---

Why It Matters

Angkor Wat matters, of course, as an aesthetic achievement — as one of the most beautiful and technically accomplished structures ever raised by human civilization. But its deeper significance lies in what it reveals about the relationship between environment, engineering, and empire.

The Khmer did not build Angkor Wat despite their environment. They built it because of their environment — because they had learned, over generations, to read and manage the hydraulic rhythms of the Tonle Sap basin with a sophistication that no other medieval civilization matched. Their engineering was not a set of isolated techniques but an integrated worldview, one in which water, rice, labor, divinity, and stone were all part of the same system. When that system came under stress — from climate, from war, from political fragmentation — the city faltered even as the temple endured.

This is a story that resonates uncomfortably in the twenty-first century, when we are learning again, slowly and painfully, that civilizations are not separate from their environments but expressions of them. The Khmer built one of the world's great cities on a foundation of hydraulic mastery and agricultural surplus. When the water system began to fail, the city could not be sustained. The temple, made of stone, outlasted the civilization that made it — as monuments always do.

There is also something worth sitting with in the sheer human scale of the achievement. Tens of thousands of people, working without modern machinery, coordinated across decades to produce something that still stops visitors cold nine centuries later. They did it within a system of belief that told them the work was sacred, that the mountain they were building connected earth to heaven. Whether or not you share that belief, it is impossible to stand in Angkor Wat's galleries and not feel the weight of that conviction — pressed into every stone.
