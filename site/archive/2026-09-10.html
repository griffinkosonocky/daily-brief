The Bones That Spoke: The Shang Dynasty and the Origins of Chinese Writing

The Daily Brief — Ancient History | East Asia

---

A Discovery in the Apothecary

In the winter of 1899, a scholar named Wang Yirong was suffering from malaria in Beijing. His physician prescribed, as Chinese pharmacists had for centuries, a remedy that included powdered "dragon bones" — fragments of ancient turtle shells and animal bones ground into medicinal dust. Wang, a learned man and an expert in ancient bronzes, happened to examine the bones before they were crushed. He noticed something extraordinary: scratched into their surfaces were marks that looked, unmistakably, like writing.

What Wang Yirong had stumbled upon were oracle bones — the preserved divination records of the Shang dynasty, buried for over three thousand years beneath the fields of a small village in Henan Province called Xiaotun. He began purchasing every bone he could find. He died the following year, a casualty of the Boxer Rebellion, but the discovery he had made would ultimately transform our understanding of China's ancient past and reveal, in cracked and scorched fragments, the earliest known ancestor of the writing system used by more than a billion people today.

The bones had been speaking all along. The world simply hadn't known how to listen.

---

The World Before the Writing

To understand what the Shang dynasty meant, it helps to understand the silence that preceded it — not an empty silence, but an inarticulate one, a world whose complexity we can only partially recover.

By the time the Shang rose to dominance in the Yellow River valley, human beings had been farming the North China Plain for thousands of years. The Neolithic cultures of the region — the Yangshao, with their painted pottery and millet fields; the Longshan, with their astonishing eggshell-thin black ceramics and their rammed-earth settlements — had already built the social scaffolding upon which Bronze Age civilization would climb. They had villages, craft specialization, trade networks, and almost certainly ritual hierarchies. What they did not have, as far as we know, was writing.

This absence is worth sitting with. Complex societies can function without writing for a very long time. Memory, oral tradition, knotted cords, tally sticks — human beings are remarkably inventive about encoding information in the absence of script. But writing changes something fundamental. It externalizes memory. It allows the dead to speak to the living. It permits a ruler to issue commands that travel farther than any messenger's voice. It creates, in short, the infrastructure of a certain kind of power.

The Shang dynasty, which most historians place between roughly 1600 and 1046 BCE, was that kind of power. And the writing it produced was not, as we shall see, invented to record poetry or commerce. It was invented, in its earliest surviving form, to ask questions of the divine.

---

The Shang World

Picture the Yellow River valley in the fourteenth century BCE. The river itself is not yet the sorrow-laden, flood-prone watercourse it would become after millennia of deforestation and siltation. The landscape is wetter, more forested, home to elephants and rhinoceroses whose bones have been found in Shang archaeological layers — animals now associated with tropical Asia, not northern China. The climate is warmer. The land is rich.

At the center of this world sits a city the Shang called, in their own records, simply "the Great Settlement Shang" — the place archaeologists now identify as Yinxu, near the modern city of Anyang in Henan Province. It is a capital of considerable scale: royal palaces on tamped-earth foundations, massive royal tombs dug deep into the loess, workshops producing bronze vessels of extraordinary technical sophistication, and all around, the bones of thousands of sacrificed humans and animals.

The Shang kings ruled through a combination of military power, ritual authority, and ancestral prestige. Their world was saturated with the sacred. The spirits of royal ancestors were believed to be active intermediaries between the living and a high deity the Shang called Di — a supreme power who controlled rain, harvests, military outcomes, and the fate of the dynasty itself. The king's primary function was not administrative in any modern sense. He was, above all, a ritual specialist, a man whose job was to maintain communication between the human and divine realms.

And this is where the writing comes in.

---

The Oracle and the Bone

The process of Shang divination was elaborate and precise. A diviner — sometimes the king himself, sometimes a specialist — would take a piece of turtle plastron (the flat underside of a turtle shell) or a cattle scapula (a shoulder blade) and prepare it carefully: scraping it smooth, drilling rows of oval hollows into its surface to weaken the material at specific points. Then, at the moment of divination, a heated bronze rod would be pressed into one of these hollows. The bone would crack with a sharp sound, and the pattern of the crack — its direction, its length, its relationship to the drilled hollow — would be read as the answer from the ancestral spirits.

What makes these objects so extraordinary is what happened next. The questions asked, and often the answers received, were inscribed on the bone itself. In a script that is recognizably the ancestor of modern Chinese characters, the diviners recorded the charge — the statement of what was being divined — along with the date, the name of the diviner, and sometimes a verification: a note, added later, confirming whether the oracle had proved correct.

"On the day jiashen, the king divined: 'In the next ten days there will be no disasters.' In the eighth day, guisi, there really was a great wind."

That sentence — or something like it — was written three thousand years ago by a human being who was worried about the coming week. The intimacy of it is startling. These are not monuments or proclamations. They are the anxious, meticulous records of people trying to know what tomorrow would bring.

The questions range across the full texture of Shang royal life. Will the harvest be good? Will the queen give birth safely — and will it be a son? Should the king go hunting on this day or that? Will the military campaign to the west succeed? What sacrifice does the ancestor Fu Hao require this month? How many cattle, how many sheep, how many human prisoners should be offered to appease the spirits of the royal dead?

Tens of thousands of these inscribed bones have been recovered. They constitute the largest single archive of early Chinese writing, and through them, the Shang dynasty has become one of the best-documented Bronze Age civilizations in the world.

---

The Script Itself: A Mystery with Deep Roots

Here is where the scholarly debates become genuinely fascinating — and genuinely unresolved.

The writing on the oracle bones is already, by any measure, a mature system. It has hundreds of distinct graphs. It combines logographic elements (signs that represent words or morphemes) with phonetic components (signs that indicate sound). It has internal logic, consistent conventions, and the clear hallmarks of a system that has been in use and development for some time before the earliest bones we have found.

This raises an obvious and vexing question: where did it come from?

Some scholars have pointed to possible antecedents in the pottery marks of the Neolithic Longshan culture, or in carved signs on bones from even earlier sites. Others have proposed that writing developed in China independently, with no connection to the writing systems emerging around the same period in Mesopotamia and Egypt. The independent invention hypothesis is taken seriously by most specialists, partly because the Chinese script's structure is so distinctive — it did not develop an alphabet, and its logic is fundamentally different from cuneiform or hieroglyphics — and partly because there is no credible evidence of contact or transmission across the distances involved.

But "independent invention" does not mean "sudden invention." The frustrating reality is that we do not have the intermediate stages. We have the mature oracle bone script, and we have scattered earlier marks that may or may not be proto-writing, and between them there is a gap that archaeology has not yet filled. It is entirely possible — many scholars believe probable — that writing developed in China on perishable materials (bamboo, wood, silk) long before anyone thought to inscribe turtle shells, and that the oracle bones represent a late and specialized branch of a longer tradition. If so, the actual origin of Chinese writing may be permanently beyond our reach.

What we can say with confidence is this: by the time of the late Shang dynasty, roughly 1200 BCE, the Chinese writing system was already sophisticated, flexible, and deeply embedded in the ritual and administrative life of the state. That is remarkable enough.

---

The Fall and the Survival

In 1046 BCE — the date is now fixed with reasonable confidence through astronomical records — the Shang dynasty fell. A coalition of western peoples led by the Zhou defeated the last Shang king, a man whose reputation for cruelty and excess may be partly historical and partly the propaganda of his conquerors, at the Battle of Muye. The Shang state collapsed. The royal house was extinguished or absorbed. The great city of Yinxu was abandoned.

And the oracle bones were buried, and forgotten, and eventually the pharmacists found them.

But the writing did not die. The Zhou, who replaced the Shang, inherited the script. They adapted it, expanded it, and used it for new purposes — not only ritual divination but administrative record-keeping, literature, philosophy, and history. The bronze vessels of the Zhou period bear long inscriptions recording royal grants and military victories. By the time of the Warring States period, several centuries later, writing had become the medium of China's great intellectual flowering: the texts of Confucius, Laozi, Mencius, Sunzi, and dozens of other thinkers who would define Chinese civilization for millennia.

The script continued to evolve — from oracle bone script through bronze inscriptions, through the seal script of the Qin dynasty, through clerical script, through the standard script that is still recognizable in modern Chinese characters. It was standardized, simplified, spread across an empire, and eventually transmitted to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, where adapted versions of the script became the foundations of entirely distinct literary traditions.

The line from a diviner pressing a hot rod against a turtle shell in fourteenth-century BCE Anyang to a student writing Chinese characters today is long, but it is unbroken. That is an extraordinary thing.

---

Why It Matters

There is a temptation, when reading about ancient writing systems, to treat them as curiosities — clever solutions to practical problems, or windows into exotic ritual worlds. But the story of the Shang oracle bones and the origins of Chinese writing touches something more durable and more human than that.

Writing is how civilizations remember. It is how they argue with themselves across time, how the dead instruct the living, how a set of values or a body of knowledge survives the death of every individual who holds it. The Shang diviners were not thinking about posterity when they scratched their questions into turtle shells. They were trying to manage uncertainty — the same uncertainty that confronts every human being who has ever wondered whether the harvest will come, whether the child will be born safely, whether tomorrow will be better than today.

What they left behind, accidentally, was a record of that uncertainty. And in doing so, they gave us something invaluable: the knowledge that the anxious, hopeful, practical inner life of a Bronze Age Chinese king is not entirely alien to our own.

The oracle bones also remind us that writing is never neutral. The script that developed in the Shang court was a tool of power — it encoded the king's access to the divine, it legitimized his authority, it recorded his victories. When the Zhou inherited it, they inherited not just a technology but a worldview. Every empire that subsequently used Chinese characters was, in some sense, inheriting that Shang legacy: the idea that writing connects the human to something larger than itself, that words properly arranged carry a weight that goes beyond mere communication.

In a world where the future of writing itself — its forms, its media, its purposes — is actively contested, there is something grounding about returning to its origins. People have always needed to ask their questions somewhere. The Shang asked theirs in bone and fire. We ask ours in light on screens. The questions, it turns out, are not so different.
