Scattered Like Seed: The Jewish Diaspora After the Fall of Jerusalem

Published today | Ancient History | Mediterranean & Near East

---

The Hook

Imagine standing on a hilltop east of the city, watching columns of smoke rise from the place your people had called the navel of the world. The Temple — the house of God, the axis around which an entire civilization turned — is burning. The priests are dead or fleeing. The sacred objects are being loaded onto Roman wagons to be carted back to a city on the Tiber as trophies. And you are alive, which means you are either a slave in chains or a fugitive with nothing but memory in your hands. What do you do with a religion when the building it was built around no longer exists? What do you do with a people when the land that defined them has been taken away?

This is the question that the fall of Jerusalem in 70 CE forced upon roughly five to six million Jews scattered across the ancient world. The answer they gave over the following centuries is one of the most consequential acts of cultural and spiritual reinvention in the history of humanity.

---

The World Before

To understand what was lost, you must first understand what Jerusalem was — not merely as a city, but as a concept.

By the first century CE, the Jewish world was already far larger than the land of Judea. Centuries of displacement had seen Jewish communities planted across an enormous arc of territory. There were substantial Jewish populations in Alexandria, where perhaps a million Jews lived, constituting perhaps a third of the city's population. There were communities in Antioch, in Babylon, in Cyrene, in Rome itself — the historian Philo of Alexandria estimated that there were a million Jews in Egypt alone. The Apostle Paul, writing in the 50s CE, could travel from city to city across Asia Minor and Greece and reliably find a synagogue to preach in. The diaspora, in other words, already existed before 70 CE.

But Jerusalem was the center of gravity for all of it. The Temple was not merely a local shrine. It was the one place on earth where the sacrificial rites prescribed by the Torah could be properly performed, where atonement was made, where pilgrimage brought Jews from every corner of the Mediterranean world three times a year for the great festivals. The half-shekel Temple tax was collected from Jewish communities from Spain to Persia, a kind of voluntary global levy that sent rivers of coin toward the Holy City. To be Jewish, wherever you lived, was to be oriented toward Jerusalem the way a compass needle orients toward magnetic north.

The political context of the First Jewish–Roman War (66–73 CE) is tangled and tragic. Judea had been a Roman province since 6 CE, and the relationship between the occupying power and the occupied people had deteriorated through a combination of Roman administrative incompetence, religious insensitivity, and local factionalism. The Jewish revolt that broke out in 66 CE was not a unified national uprising but a fractured, violent convulsion in which Jewish factions killed each other as readily as they fought Romans. The Sicarii, the Zealots, the followers of John of Gischala and Simon bar Giora — these groups warred within the walls of Jerusalem even as Vespasian's legions closed in from outside. Josephus, our most detailed source and himself a former rebel commander who defected to the Roman side, describes the internal slaughter with horrified fascination.

When Titus finally breached the walls in the summer of 70 CE, the city had already been weakened by famine and fratricidal bloodshed. What followed was catastrophic. Josephus records that 1.1 million people died — a figure modern scholars regard as vastly inflated, but the scale of destruction was nonetheless enormous. Hundreds of thousands were enslaved. The Temple was burned, its treasures seized, its priesthood extinguished as a functioning institution. The Arch of Titus in Rome, still standing today, shows the menorah and the Table of Showbread being carried in triumph through the streets of the capital.

---

The Story

The world that emerged from the ashes of 70 CE had to be built from almost nothing, and the people who built it were working against time, grief, and the active hostility of Rome.

The rabbis — the hakhamim, the sages — understood almost immediately that the old system was gone and that something new had to take its place. The center of gravity shifted almost overnight to a small coastal town called Yavneh, or Jamnia, where a rabbinic academy had been established, according to tradition, by Yohanan ben Zakkai. The story of how he got there is itself legendary: he reportedly had himself smuggled out of besieged Jerusalem in a coffin, gained an audience with Vespasian, and asked permission to establish a school. Whether the details are literally true or not, the story captures something real — the rabbinic leadership's pragmatic instinct for survival, its willingness to negotiate with power in order to preserve the essential.

At Yavneh, the rabbis began the enormous task of reconstituting Jewish religious life around the synagogue and the study of Torah rather than around the Temple and its sacrifices. Prayer would substitute for sacrifice. The rabbi would substitute for the priest. The home, with its Sabbath candles and its Passover seder, would become a portable temple that could be erected anywhere on earth. This was not a small adjustment. It was a fundamental reimagining of what it meant to be in relationship with God.

The decades after 70 CE were also a period of intense textual activity. The oral traditions that had accumulated around the Torah for centuries were gradually being written down and systematized. This process would eventually produce the Mishnah, compiled around 200 CE under Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi, and later the two Talmuds — the Jerusalem Talmud and the far more authoritative Babylonian Talmud, completed perhaps around 500 CE. These texts would become, in effect, the portable homeland of the Jewish people: a country made of words, carried in the minds of scholars from Baghdad to Barcelona.

But the decades between 70 and 200 CE were also marked by further catastrophe. The revolt of the diaspora Jews between 115 and 117 CE — the Kitos War — saw Jewish communities in Egypt, Cyprus, and Cyrene rise against Roman rule in a conflict of extraordinary violence. Then came the Bar Kokhba revolt of 132–135 CE, the last great attempt to reclaim Judea by military force. When Hadrian crushed it, he renamed the province Syria Palaestina, plowed Jerusalem under, and built a Roman city called Aelia Capitolina on its ruins. Jews were forbidden from entering on pain of death. The land itself was erased from the map as a Jewish homeland, at least in Roman administrative terms.

These successive catastrophes deepened the diaspora and intensified its character. The communities scattered across the Mediterranean and the Parthian east now had to develop still more robust local institutions, still more self-sufficient communal structures. The synagogue became the center of every Jewish community — a place of prayer, study, legal adjudication, and social welfare. The Jewish calendar, with its cycle of fasts and feasts, created a shared rhythm of time that could be observed identically in Rome and in Babylon, in Alexandria and in Antioch. Memory became the connective tissue of a people who could not share a geography.

The Babylonian community deserves particular attention. The Jews of Mesopotamia — heirs to the communities established after the Babylonian exile of the sixth century BCE — now found themselves living under the relatively tolerant rule of the Parthian and later Sasanian empires. Largely free from the existential pressure that Roman Jews faced, the Babylonian academies of Sura and Pumbedita became the intellectual powerhouses of the medieval Jewish world. The Babylonian Talmud that emerged from these academies would become the defining text of rabbinic Judaism, the framework within which Jewish law, ethics, and theology would be debated for the next fifteen hundred years.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Historians have argued for generations about how to interpret this period, and several genuine mysteries persist.

One concerns the nature of the Yavneh academy itself. The traditional narrative, shaped largely by rabbinic literature, presents Yavneh as the orderly successor to Jerusalem, with Yohanan ben Zakkai calmly reconstituting Jewish authority under Roman patronage. But scholars like Shaye J.D. Cohen and Seth Schwartz have questioned how much real authority the Yavneh rabbis actually exercised in the decades immediately after 70 CE. Schwartz, in his provocative Imperialism and Jewish Society, argues that rabbinic culture was in fact a minority phenomenon for much of this period, that most Jews in Roman Palestine lived in a world still heavily shaped by Hellenistic and Roman culture, and that the rabbis only achieved genuine social dominance centuries later. This is a significant revision of the traditional picture.

There is also the question of the canon. The Council of Yavneh — if it can even be called a council — has traditionally been credited with fixing the Hebrew biblical canon, deciding which books were scripture and which were not. But modern scholars are skeptical that any formal council occurred, or that the canon was settled as neatly as the story suggests. The process of canonization was almost certainly messier, more gradual, and more contested than the tidy legend implies.

Finally, there is the demographic question. How many Jews were there, and where were they? The ancient sources give wildly varying numbers, and modern scholars have produced estimates ranging from four million to eight million Jews in the first century CE, representing perhaps eight to ten percent of the Roman Empire's population. If the higher estimates are correct, the Jewish diaspora was one of the most significant demographic facts of the ancient Mediterranean world — a presence in virtually every major city, a network of communities with shared texts, shared practices, and shared memories spanning thousands of miles.

---

The Aftermath

What rose from the ruins of Jerusalem was something genuinely new: a form of religious identity that had learned to survive without a homeland, without a temple, without a priesthood, and without political sovereignty. This was not a consolation prize. It was, in retrospect, an extraordinary evolutionary achievement.

The rabbinic model proved extraordinarily durable. Jewish communities maintained their coherence through the collapse of the Roman Empire, through the rise of Christianity as the dominant religion of Europe, through the Islamic conquests that reshaped the Near East, through the Crusades, through expulsion after expulsion from country after country. Each time, the community reconstituted itself around the same core: the text, the calendar, the synagogue, the memory of Jerusalem.

The influence of this model extended far beyond Judaism itself. Christianity, which emerged from the same Jewish world and was shaped by many of the same crises, adapted many of the same tools — the scriptural canon, the weekly gathering, the communal meal with symbolic resonance. Islam, too, built institutions of textual scholarship and communal practice that bore the marks of this ancient pattern. The very idea that a religion could exist without a state, without a territory, without a single physical center of worship — this was a genuinely novel contribution to the history of human civilization, and it came out of the disaster of 70 CE.

---

Why It Matters

The fall of Jerusalem and the diaspora that followed speak to questions that have never gone away. What holds a people together when the land is taken from them? What is the relationship between identity and place, between culture and geography? How does a community preserve itself across centuries of hostility and dispersal?

The rabbis' answer — that identity could be carried in texts, in practices, in the rhythms of a shared calendar, in the act of memory itself — was a radical and enduring one. It suggests that what makes a people is not territory but story: the stories they tell about who they are, where they came from, and what they owe each other. This is not merely a Jewish insight. It is a human one, and it has been reached, in different forms, by every people who has ever been forced to survive without the ground beneath their feet.

The world is still full of such people. The question the rabbis answered in the ruins of Jerusalem — how do you carry a civilization in your hands? — remains one of the most important questions anyone can ask.
