The Daily Brief

Blood, Roses, and the Making of a Dynasty: The Wars of the Roses and the Tudor Settlement

Late Medieval England | The Daily Brief

---

The Hook

Imagine standing in a muddy field outside the small Yorkshire market town of Towton on Palm Sunday, 1461. The snow is driving sideways into your face. Around you, somewhere between fifty and eighty thousand men — the largest army ever assembled on English soil — are trying to kill one another. The battle will last ten hours. When it finally ends, somewhere between nine thousand and twenty-eight thousand men will be dead, their blood turning the snowdrifts pink, a local stream running red for days. And this is not the end of anything. It is barely the middle.

The Wars of the Roses did not announce themselves as a world-historical event. They came on gradually, like a fever — a quarrel here, a murdered noble there, a king who couldn't quite hold his kingdom together — and then, suddenly, England was consuming itself in a dynastic struggle so savage and so prolonged that it reshaped the entire political architecture of the British Isles. What emerged from the wreckage was something genuinely new: the Tudor monarchy, a political settlement so durable that its echoes still move through English institutions today.

---

The World Before

To understand what broke, you have to understand what existed before the breaking.

Medieval England operated on a principle that seems almost quaint today: the king was the keystone of everything. He was God's anointed lieutenant on earth, the fount of justice, the commander of armies, the guarantor of order. Beneath him, a web of noble families held vast estates, commanded private retinues of armed men, and administered justice across the shires. The system worked — when the king was strong enough to make it work.

The Plantagenet dynasty had, at its best moments, produced formidable rulers. Edward III had humiliated France at Crécy and Poitiers. His grandson Henry V had done the same at Agincourt and come within a whisker of uniting the English and French crowns. These were men who commanded personal loyalty through sheer force of will and military success. The nobility followed them because following them paid.

But the system had a fatal vulnerability: it could not survive a weak or incapable king. And in 1422, Henry V died of dysentery at thirty-five, leaving his throne to a nine-month-old infant. That infant, Henry VI, would grow into one of the most catastrophically unsuited monarchs in English history — pious, gentle, prone to episodes of complete mental collapse, and constitutionally incapable of the decisive, intimidating authority that medieval kingship required.

By the 1450s, England had lost almost everything Henry V had won in France. The nobility, enriched by decades of French war, were increasingly unmanageable. The royal finances were a disaster. The court was faction-ridden, dominated by the king's formidable French queen, Margaret of Anjou, and her allies. And sitting at the edge of this deteriorating situation was Richard, Duke of York — a man with an arguably better claim to the throne than Henry himself, and the military prestige and landed wealth to make that claim dangerous.

---

The Story

The conflict that historians would later, with a certain romantic tidiness, call the Wars of the Roses began not with a battle but with a series of political humiliations, failed reconciliations, and escalating acts of violence that made peaceful resolution increasingly impossible.

The roses themselves — the red rose of Lancaster, the white rose of York — are largely a retrospective myth, a piece of Tudor-era branding that Shakespeare enthusiastically embellished. Contemporaries sometimes used floral badges, but the neat opposition of red and white was imposed after the fact, once the Tudors needed a tidy story about the chaos they had ended. The reality was murkier, more personal, and more contingent.

The first open battle came at St Albans in 1455, a brief, sharp affair in which Yorkist forces ambushed the Lancastrian court and killed several of the king's chief ministers. Henry VI himself was slightly wounded and briefly captured. It looked, for a moment, like a political coup rather than a war — York took control of the government, Henry remained king, and there was talk of reconciliation. But Margaret of Anjou, fighting for her son's inheritance with a ferocity that contemporaries found both admirable and terrifying, would not accept the settlement. She worked tirelessly to rebuild Lancastrian power, and in 1459 the war resumed in earnest.

What followed over the next thirty years was a dizzying sequence of battles, reversals, and unexpected resurrections. York himself was killed at the Battle of Wakefield in December 1460, his head displayed on the gates of York wearing a paper crown — a piece of theatrical cruelty that his enemies would come to regret. His son Edward, eighteen years old and already a military prodigy, took up the cause with cold fury.

It was Edward who won at Towton, that catastrophic Palm Sunday slaughter in the snow, and who rode south to be crowned Edward IV. He was everything his father had hoped to be: tall, handsome, charismatic, politically shrewd, and genuinely terrifying in battle. For most of the 1460s, it looked as though the Yorkists had won.

But the story wasn't finished. Edward fell out spectacularly with his greatest ally, the Earl of Warwick — a man so powerful that later generations called him "the Kingmaker" — and the resulting breach produced one of the most extraordinary episodes in medieval English history: Warwick switched sides, allied with the exiled Margaret of Anjou, and briefly restored Henry VI to the throne in 1470. Henry was paraded through London looking, by contemporary accounts, dazed and ill. Edward fled to the continent.

He came back the following spring, won two decisive battles in rapid succession — Barnet and Tewkesbury — and the Lancastrian cause effectively died with them. Warwick was killed at Barnet. Henry VI's son Edward, Prince of Wales, was killed at Tewkesbury. And Henry VI himself died in the Tower of London a few days later, almost certainly murdered. Edward IV ruled unchallenged for the next twelve years.

His death in 1483 — unexpected, at forty, probably from pneumonia following a fishing trip — reopened everything. His young son, twelve-year-old Edward V, was intercepted before he could reach London by his uncle, Richard Duke of Gloucester. Within weeks, Richard had declared his nephews illegitimate, placed them in the Tower, and crowned himself Richard III. The two boys — the Princes in the Tower — were never seen in public again.

Richard III is one of English history's great contested figures, his reputation shaped for centuries by Tudor propaganda and Shakespeare's magnificent, gleefully malevolent dramatic portrait. He may have been a capable administrator and a brave soldier. He was also a man who had, by any reasonable reading of the evidence, seized the throne by questionable means and allowed — at minimum — his nephews to disappear. His support was never secure.

In August 1485, a Welsh-born claimant with a thin but uncontested Lancastrian bloodline landed in Wales with a small French-backed army. His name was Henry Tudor. At the Battle of Bosworth Field, Richard III charged directly at Henry's position and was killed — the last English king to die in battle. Henry rode away as Henry VII.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Historians have argued for generations about what the Wars of the Roses actually were. Were they a genuine constitutional crisis rooted in structural problems with late medieval English kingship? A simple dynastic accident that might not have happened with a competent Henry VI? An early symptom of the broader crisis of feudal power structures across Europe?

The fate of the Princes in the Tower remains perhaps the most emotionally charged cold case in English history. Richard III almost certainly had both motive and opportunity. But no bodies were definitively identified until 1674, when bones were discovered during construction at the Tower, and even those bones have been disputed. Modern DNA analysis has been proposed but not yet conclusively conducted. The mystery endures.

There is also a lively debate about how "devastating" the wars actually were for England. Older historiography painted them as a catastrophic bloodbath that depopulated the nobility and traumatized the country. More recent scholarship, particularly work by historians like Christine Carpenter and John Watts, has emphasized that most of England most of the time was not directly touched by the fighting, that trade and agriculture continued, and that the wars were devastating primarily for the nobility rather than the population at large. This is a more nuanced picture, though Towton alone — whatever the exact numbers — was a genuine catastrophe by any measure.

---

The Aftermath: The Tudor Settlement

Henry VII was, by any standard, a remarkable political operator. He had no military glamour, no personal charisma to speak of, and a claim to the throne that required some creative genealogical argument. What he had was an extraordinary talent for institutional consolidation.

He married Elizabeth of York — Edward IV's daughter — and thereby united the two rival claims in a single household. The Tudor rose, combining red and white, was the dynasty's central propaganda image, and it was brilliantly effective. Henry moved systematically to break the power of the over-mighty nobility that had made the wars possible, using legal mechanisms — the Court of Star Chamber, bonds and recognizances that could ruin a noble financially if he stepped out of line — to enforce a new kind of royal authority that didn't depend on personal military dominance.

He rebuilt the royal finances with a thoroughness that earned him a reputation for avarice but left his son a treasury that made the English Renaissance possible. He kept England out of expensive foreign wars. He negotiated a marriage alliance with Spain — his son Arthur to Catherine of Aragon — that positioned England as a significant player in European diplomacy.

When he died in 1509, the succession passed peacefully to his second son, Henry VIII. The transition was so smooth it was almost unremarkable. After eighty years of dynastic chaos, that smoothness was itself a kind of miracle.

Henry VIII, of course, created his own chaos — but it was a different kind of chaos, religious and institutional rather than purely dynastic, and it ultimately produced the Church of England, the English Reformation, and the long chain of consequences that runs through Elizabeth I's reign, the English Civil War, the Glorious Revolution, and deep into modernity.

---

Why It Matters

The Wars of the Roses matter beyond their drama — and the drama is considerable — because they illuminate something enduring about the relationship between political legitimacy and institutional stability.

Medieval England's problem was that it had built its entire political order around the person of the king, with almost no institutional buffers when that person failed. When the king was strong, the system worked brilliantly. When he was weak, or mad, or a child, everything descended into violence. The Tudor settlement's great achievement was to begin building something more durable: a monarchy that was still personal but was increasingly backed by law, by bureaucracy, by Parliament, and by a national Church that tied religious legitimacy to the crown itself.

This is a story that repeats across history with painful regularity. Societies that concentrate power in persons rather than institutions are always one bad leader away from catastrophe. The institutions that seem like constraints on power are actually what make power stable and transferable. Henry VII understood this instinctively, even if he couldn't have articulated it in those terms.

There is also something worth sitting with in the sheer contingency of it all. Henry Tudor's claim to the throne was genuinely weak. He won at Bosworth partly because key Yorkist nobles — the Stanley family, crucially — switched sides at the decisive moment. A slightly different set of decisions, and England might have had another decade of Yorkist rule, a different Reformation or none at all, a different relationship with Scotland and Ireland and Europe.

History is not inevitable. The roses could have bloomed differently. That they bloomed into the Tudors was partly skill, partly ruthlessness, and partly the kind of luck that only reveals itself in retrospect.
