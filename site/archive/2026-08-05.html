Fire in the Corn: The Mexican Revolution and the Birth of Modern Mexico

---

Tuesday Morning Read · Era: Modern · Region: North America

---

The Hook

On the morning of November 20, 1910, a schoolteacher and idealist named Francisco Madero was supposed to launch a revolution. He had printed the pamphlets, set the date, written the manifesto. Instead, he sat trembling in San Antonio, Texas, a fugitive from his own uprising, while across the border in the state of Chihuahua, a cattle rancher named Pascual Orozco and a bandit-turned-general named Pancho Villa decided they would start the war themselves. They didn't wait for permission. They rarely would again.

What followed over the next decade was not a clean revolution with a single villain and a single hero. It was something stranger and more human than that — a volcanic eruption of a country that had been pressurized for thirty years under the boot of one man, releasing everything at once: peasant rage, regional pride, indigenous memory, foreign greed, and the desperate hunger for land. It would kill somewhere between one and two million people. It would write one of the most radical constitutions in the world. And it would invent a mythology of itself so powerful that Mexico is still living inside it today.

---

The World Before: The Long Dictatorship

To understand why Mexico exploded, you have to understand what it was before it did.

Porfirio Díaz had ruled Mexico since 1876, with one brief interruption, in a system so total and so personalized that Mexicans simply called it El Porfiriato — the Porfirian age, as if the country had become an extension of one man's will. Díaz was in many ways a product of genuine achievement. He had fought the French intervention, served under Benito Juárez, and genuinely believed that Mexico needed order before it could have anything else. He delivered order. He also delivered something close to a cage.

Under Díaz, Mexico modernized at a breathtaking pace that almost entirely bypassed the people who actually lived there. Foreign capital — British, American, French — poured into railroads, mines, oil fields, and haciendas. By 1910, American companies alone owned more land in Mexico than Mexican citizens did. The rail network expanded from 400 miles to nearly 15,000. Cities lit up with electricity. A small, Europeanized elite called the científicos — technocrats who believed that science, positivism, and racial hierarchy could be engineered into national progress — guided policy from Mexico City and sent their children to Paris.

Meanwhile, in the villages of Morelos, Oaxaca, Guerrero, and Chihuahua, something very different was happening. The hacienda system had swallowed millions of acres of communal indigenous land. Peasant farmers who had worked the same earth for generations found themselves landless laborers on their own ancestral ground, paid in company-store scrip, bound by debt peonage that their grandchildren would inherit. In Morelos alone, the sugar haciendas had consumed so much land and water that entire villages had been functionally erased. A man named Emiliano Zapata, who worked horses on one of those haciendas, watched this happen and remembered every acre.

Díaz was 80 years old in 1910. He gave an interview to an American journalist named James Creelman in which he seemed to invite political opposition, suggesting Mexico was ready for democracy. It was almost certainly a performance for foreign audiences. But Francisco Madero, the slight, vegetarian, spiritualist son of a wealthy northern family, took it seriously. He ran for president. Díaz had him arrested, declared himself the winner, and released Madero — who then fled to Texas and wrote The Plan of San Luis Potosí, calling for revolution on November 20, 1910.

The date passed quietly in Texas. In Chihuahua, it did not.

---

The Revolution Itself: A Story in Four Acts

Act One: The Fall of the Old Regime (1910–1911)

The revolution's first phase was swift and almost shockingly successful. Pancho Villa, whose real name was Doroteo Arango and whose biography included cattle rustling, banditry, and a possibly invented story about avenging a sister's honor, threw himself into the fight with the kind of physical charisma that turned men into followers. Pascual Orozco led guerrilla raids across Chihuahua. In Morelos, Emiliano Zapata — quiet, dark-eyed, with an extraordinary mustache and an absolute moral clarity about land — organized the peasants of his state into a fighting force.

By May 1911, the federal fortress of Ciudad Juárez had fallen to Villa and Orozco. Díaz, elderly and suffering from an infected tooth, resigned and sailed to Paris, where he would die in exile four years later. Madero rode into Mexico City in triumph. Enormous crowds filled the streets. People wept.

The euphoria lasted about fifteen minutes in historical terms.

Act Two: Betrayal and Chaos (1911–1913)

Madero was a genuine democrat and a genuine disappointment. He believed in elections and procedure at a moment when the country needed land reform and structural transformation. He kept Díaz's old army officers in place, tried to negotiate with Zapata rather than give him land, and found himself squeezed between a right that hated him for being too radical and a left that hated him for not being radical enough.

Zapata, who had initially supported Madero, issued his own manifesto — The Plan of Ayala — in November 1911, declaring Madero a traitor and calling for the immediate redistribution of land. "The land belongs to those who work it" was not merely a slogan; it was a statement of cosmology. For Zapata and the Zapatistas, land was identity, ancestry, and survival. It was not negotiable.

Then came February 1913 and the Decena Trágica — the Ten Tragic Days. A military coup, engineered with the knowledge (and likely encouragement) of American Ambassador Henry Lane Wilson, placed General Victoriano Huerta in power. Madero and his vice president were arrested, and days later, murdered — officially described as an escape attempt, a story that convinced no one. Huerta was brutal, alcoholic, and deeply conservative. He was also the thing that united everyone against him.

Act Three: The Revolution Devours Itself (1913–1915)

What followed was the revolution's most violent and most mythologized phase. Three major forces converged against Huerta: Venustiano Carranza, a wealthy rancher and former senator who claimed constitutional legitimacy; Pancho Villa, now commanding the legendary División del Norte with tens of thousands of men, artillery, and his own personal train; and Emiliano Zapata in the south. A fourth figure, Álvaro Obregón, led Constitutionalist armies from Sonora with a cold tactical brilliance that would eventually prove decisive.

The battles of 1914 were enormous by any standard — Villa's assault on Zacatecas in June 1914 involved 23,000 men and killed more than 7,000 in a single day. Huerta fled. The victors entered Mexico City.

Then they turned on each other.

The Convention of Aguascalientes in late 1914, meant to unify the revolution, instead clarified its fractures. Villa and Zapata, the two great popular commanders, briefly allied against Carranza. They met in Xochimilco and then, famously, entered Mexico City together in December 1914 — Villa in a general's uniform, Zapata in his silver-studded charro outfit, sitting side by side in the National Palace for a photograph that became one of the most iconic images in Mexican history. But the alliance was uneasy, and neither man was built for the politics of governing a capital.

Obregón, fighting for Carranza, proved to be a military genius who had studied the trench warfare of World War One. At the Battle of Celaya in April 1915, he dug trenches, strung barbed wire, and let Villa's cavalry charges break against them repeatedly. Villa lost perhaps 4,000 men in two days. He never fully recovered as a military force.

Act Four: Constitution and Assassination (1916–1920)

With Villa broken and Zapata confined to guerrilla resistance in Morelos, Carranza consolidated power and convened a constitutional congress at Querétaro in 1916–1917. The delegates he assembled were more radical than he intended. They produced a document — the Constitution of 1917 — that was startling in its ambitions: Article 27 declared all subsoil resources the property of the nation, a direct challenge to foreign oil companies; Article 123 established an eight-hour workday, the right to strike, and minimum wages; Article 3 established secular public education. It was, in many ways, the revolution's most lasting achievement.

Zapata was lured into a trap and shot by Carrancista forces in April 1919. Villa survived until 1923, when he was ambushed in Parral — likely with the knowledge of Obregón, who had by then become president. Carranza himself was killed in 1920, fleeing a coup by Obregón's forces. The revolution was eating its founders.

---

The Scholarly Debate: What Kind of Revolution Was It?

Historians have argued for a century about what the Mexican Revolution actually was. The official story, enshrined by the ruling Partido Revolucionario Institucional (PRI) that governed Mexico for most of the 20th century, presented it as a unified nationalist uprising that produced a progressive state. The reality was considerably messier.

Alan Knight, whose two-volume The Mexican Revolution remains the most comprehensive scholarly treatment in English, argues that the revolution was genuinely popular in character — driven by real peasant and working-class grievances — but that its outcomes were shaped and ultimately constrained by the middle-class and regional elites who captured the state. The Zapatista vision of agrarian communalism was partially absorbed, partially suppressed, and largely transformed into a bureaucratic land-reform program that served political control as much as peasant welfare.

Others, particularly revisionist historians writing from the 1980s onward, have questioned how "revolutionary" the outcomes truly were. The oil nationalization of 1938 under Lázaro Cárdenas was genuinely radical. But the land reform was slow, incomplete, and frequently reversed. The PRI's "revolutionary" legitimacy became a tool of authoritarian one-party rule that lasted until 2000.

There is also the enduring mystery of what might have been. Zapata's Plan of Ayala remains one of the most coherent statements of indigenous agrarian rights ever written in the Americas. What Mexico might have looked like had his vision, rather than Carranza's, prevailed is a question that Mexican intellectuals, artists, and the Zapatista movement that took his name again in 1994 have never stopped asking.

---

The Aftermath: What Rose from the Ruins

The revolution's most immediate legacy was demographic catastrophe. Between combat deaths, the influenza pandemic of 1918, famine, and emigration, Mexico lost somewhere between 1.4 and 2.2 million people from a pre-war population of roughly 15 million. Some regions of Morelos lost a third of their inhabitants.

From this devastation, something extraordinary emerged culturally. The 1920s saw the government commission Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco, and David Alfaro Siqueiros to paint the walls of public buildings with murals depicting Mexican history — indigenous civilizations, the conquest, the revolution — in a visual language that was simultaneously Marxist, nationalist, and deeply beautiful. This muralism movement gave Mexico a public art that was explicitly for the people who walked past it every day.

The Constitution of 1917 became a template for social-democratic constitutionalism across Latin America. The oil nationalization of 1938, when President Cárdenas expelled foreign oil companies and created Pemex, was a defining act of economic nationalism that still shapes Mexican identity. Land reform, however imperfect, redistributed millions of acres and created the ejido system of communal landholding.

The PRI, which formalized the revolution's institutions into a single party in 1929, governed Mexico under a system of controlled democracy — regular elections, regular PRI victories — until Vicente Fox broke the streak in 2000. The revolution's language, symbols, and heroes remained the official vocabulary of Mexican politics long after the revolution's ideals had been hollowed out.

---

Why It Matters

The Mexican Revolution sits at the intersection of nearly every durable tension in modern history: the collision between foreign capital and national sovereignty, between elite reformism and popular radicalism, between the hunger for land and the logic of industrial development. It was one of the first great revolutions of the 20th century, preceding the Russian Revolution by seven years, and it asked questions that century would never stop asking.

It matters because it shows us that revolutions rarely deliver what their most idealistic participants imagined. Zapata died without his land reform. Madero died for believing in elections. The men who survived and built institutions were usually not the men who had the purest vision of what those institutions should be. That gap — between the revolution and the state that claims its inheritance — is one of the defining tragedies of modern political life, visible from Mexico City to Moscow to Cairo.

It matters, too, because the revolution's unfinished business keeps returning. When indigenous Zapatistas in Chiapas launched their uprising on January 1, 1994 — the day NAFTA took effect — they named themselves after Emiliano Zapata deliberately. They were saying: the questions he asked in 1911 have not been answered. The land still matters. The people who work it still matter. That argument is 115 years old and it is not over.
