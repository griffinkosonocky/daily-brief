The Black Death and the Remaking of Eurasia

Medieval History | Eurasia

---

The Smell Before the Ships

In the autumn of 1347, twelve Genoese trading galleys docked at the port of Messina, Sicily. The harbor officials who walked out to meet them made a discovery so disturbing that they ordered the ships immediately back to sea — but it was already too late. Most of the sailors aboard were dead. Those still living were covered in mysterious black swellings at the armpits and groin, weeping blood and pus, their skin mottled with dark blotches as though bruised from within. The Messinese drove the ships away. They burned the bedding, the clothing, the dockside crates. None of it mattered. Something had come ashore with those sailors, something invisible, patient, and almost incomprehensibly lethal. Within months, it would be moving north through Italy. Within three years, it would have killed between a third and a half of every person in Europe. Within a generation, it would have fundamentally altered the civilization of an entire continent — and, as historians have come to understand more fully, of the entire Eurasian world.

---

The World Before

To appreciate what the plague destroyed, you have to understand what it found.

The mid-fourteenth century was not a world in decline, at least not obviously. The great cathedrals of Europe were still being raised, stone by stone, toward heaven. The Hanseatic League had knitted the trading cities of northern Europe into a commercial web of remarkable sophistication. In the Italian city-states, the first stirrings of what would eventually be called the Renaissance were already visible — Giotto had died just a decade before the plague arrived, leaving behind frescoes that depicted human faces with a naturalism unseen since antiquity. In China, the Yuan dynasty, established by the Mongols, presided over a civilization of staggering wealth and technical ingenuity. The Silk Road — that great artery of goods, ideas, and microbes connecting East Asia to the Mediterranean — was more open and more traveled than it had been in centuries.

That openness was precisely the problem.

The Mongol conquests of the thirteenth century had, paradoxically, created something like a globalized medieval world. From the Pacific coast of China to the steppes of Central Asia, from Persia to the Black Sea, Mongol rule meant relatively safe passage for merchants, diplomats, and missionaries. Goods flowed: silk and porcelain westward, silver and horses eastward. And along those same routes, in the fleas that rode the rats that traveled in the grain sacks, moved Yersinia pestis — a bacterium that had been lurking in the rodent populations of Central Asia for centuries, waiting.

Europe, meanwhile, was already under strain in ways that would make it catastrophically vulnerable. The Medieval Warm Period, which had allowed population growth and agricultural expansion for three centuries, was ending. The Great Famine of 1315–1322 had killed perhaps ten percent of northern Europe's population. People were crowded, malnourished, and living in intimate proximity with rats. The immune systems of an entire civilization had been worn thin.

---

The Story of the Pestilence

The plague almost certainly originated, in its fourteenth-century form, somewhere in the region of Central Asia — possibly near Lake Issyk-Kul in what is now Kyrgyzstan, where Nestorian Christian gravestones from the late 1330s record an unusual spike in deaths, some inscribed with the word for "pestilence." From there, it moved with the rhythms of Mongol trade and warfare, westward through the steppes, arriving at the Crimean port of Caffa — a Genoese trading colony on the Black Sea — by 1346.

The siege of Caffa offers one of history's most chilling episodes. A Mongol army under the command of Janibeg Khan had surrounded the city, but plague broke out in the besieging force. According to the Genoese lawyer Gabriele de' Mussis, who wrote an account of the events, Janibeg's men began catapulting their plague-dead over the city walls — one of the earliest recorded instances of biological warfare. Whether this actually transmitted the disease to the Genoese inside (modern epidemiologists are skeptical that corpses alone could have caused an outbreak) or whether the rats simply moved freely between the camps, the result was the same. The Genoese fled by ship, carrying their invisible cargo across the Black Sea and into the Mediterranean.

From Sicily, the plague moved with terrible efficiency. It reached Genoa and Venice by January 1348, Florence and Paris by spring, England by the summer. It did not move in a straight line — it flickered and jumped, following trade routes, river valleys, and pilgrimage paths. Some towns it bypassed entirely, for reasons still not fully understood. Others it annihilated. Florence lost perhaps sixty percent of its population. The Florentine writer Giovanni Boccaccio, who survived, described bodies piled in the streets, parents abandoning children, the complete dissolution of social bonds: "Such was the multitude of corpses brought to the churches every day and almost every hour that there was not enough consecrated ground to give them burial."

The symptoms were awful and swift. The bubonic form — the most common — produced those characteristic swellings, called buboes, which could grow to the size of an egg. It killed within days. The pneumonic form, which spread through the air and attacked the lungs, was even faster and essentially always fatal. The septicemic form, which infected the bloodstream directly, could kill within hours, before any external symptoms appeared. Medieval physicians were helpless. Their medical theory, rooted in Galenic ideas about "miasmas" — bad air — led them to light fires, carry posies of flowers, and flee. Fleeing, of course, spread the disease further.

The plague did not spare the powerful. It killed kings and bishops, abbots and merchants. Alfonso XI of Castile died of it. So did the Archbishop of Canterbury — twice, as the plague swept through in successive waves. The psychological impact was profound and strange. Some communities turned to flagellation, whipping themselves through the streets in acts of collective penance, convinced that God was punishing humanity for its sins. Others turned on Jews, accusing them of poisoning wells, and massacred Jewish communities across the Rhineland and beyond — one of the worst episodes of anti-Semitic violence in European history before the twentieth century, a catastrophe layered atop catastrophe.

---

The Scholarly Debate

The Black Death is one of the most studied events in human history, yet it retains genuine mysteries.

The most fundamental question — what exactly caused it — was only answered in the modern era. Yersinia pestis was identified in 1894 during an outbreak in Hong Kong, and for most of the twentieth century it was assumed to be the culprit behind the medieval pandemic. But a minority of scholars, including the historian Samuel Cohn, have argued that the medieval disease spread too fast and killed too efficiently to be the same pathogen as modern bubonic plague, which requires a rat die-off as a prerequisite and spreads more slowly. Cohn and others proposed that the Black Death might have been a hemorrhagic fever of some kind, something more like Ebola.

The debate shifted dramatically in the 2010s, when ancient DNA analysis of teeth from confirmed plague burial pits in London, Germany, and France definitively identified Yersinia pestis in the remains. The bacterium was real. But the questions didn't entirely disappear — why did the medieval strain kill so much faster and more broadly than modern plague? The current best answer involves a combination of factors: a more virulent strain of the bacterium, a population with no prior immunity, catastrophic malnutrition, and secondary infections enabled by the breakdown of social order. The science and the history are still in productive conversation.

There is also the question of scale. The figure of "one-third to one-half" of Europe's population is itself contested. Some regional studies suggest mortality rates were lower; others suggest they were higher. And the question of how the plague affected Asia — where the historical record is thinner and the archaeology of plague burial less developed — remains genuinely open. China's population fell dramatically in the fourteenth century, but how much was plague versus Mongol warfare versus famine is difficult to disentangle.

---

The Aftermath: A New World Built on Bones

The survivors inherited a transformed world, and in ways that would have surprised them, many of the transformations were not entirely grim.

Labor, suddenly, was scarce. Where peasants had once competed for land and scraped by on subsistence wages, the survivors found themselves with leverage they had never possessed. Landlords across England, France, and Italy found that their surviving serfs were walking off their estates, moving to cities, demanding wages, refusing the old obligations. The English Parliament passed the Statute of Laborers in 1351, desperately trying to freeze wages at pre-plague levels. It failed. The economic historian Robert Brenner and others have traced the effective end of English serfdom to the demographic catastrophe of the plague, a process that took decades but was fundamentally set in motion by the sudden and drastic reduction in the supply of human beings.

In the cities, the disruption was equally profound. The Church, which had failed so visibly to either explain or prevent the catastrophe, lost a measure of the absolute moral authority it had previously commanded. The flagellant movements, the popular mysticism, the wave of vernacular religious writing — all of these were symptoms of a population looking for spiritual meaning outside institutional channels. Scholars have traced a line, however crooked, from the spiritual crisis of the plague years to the Wycliffite and Hussite reform movements of the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries, and from there to the Protestant Reformation of the sixteenth.

The plague also remade art. The danse macabre — the Dance of Death — became one of the defining visual motifs of the late medieval period, skeleton figures leading popes and emperors, merchants and peasants, in an equalizing procession toward the grave. Tomb sculpture changed: where wealthy patrons had once been depicted in serene, idealized repose, they now commissioned transi tombs showing their bodies as rotting cadavers. There is something almost modern in this confrontation with mortality, this refusal to look away from the body's dissolution. Some historians see in it one of the psychological preconditions for Renaissance humanism — a new urgency around the question of what it means to be alive.

The plague returned, in waves, throughout the rest of the fourteenth century and beyond. The second outbreak, in 1361, was called the "plague of children" because it killed the young who had been born after 1347 and had no acquired immunity. Europe's population would not recover to its pre-plague levels until the sixteenth century — two hundred years of demographic shadow.

---

Why It Matters

The Black Death is not merely a medieval horror story. It is a case study in how catastrophic disruption, however devastating in the moment, can reshape the structures of power, labor, and belief in ways that outlast the catastrophe itself.

The plague broke the back of feudal labor arrangements in much of Western Europe, setting in motion a slow revolution in the relationship between working people and those who employed them. It demonstrated, brutally, that institutions claiming divine authority — the Church, the monarchy — could not protect their people from nature's indifference, a lesson that echoed through centuries of subsequent reform movements. It showed how a connected world, one made prosperous by trade and movement, is also a world made vulnerable by the same connections — a truth that the twenty-first century has had occasion to relearn.

And perhaps most profoundly, it raises the question that haunts every generation that has survived a mass death event: what do you do with the world that remains? The survivors of the Black Death did not simply mourn and rebuild what had been. They built something different — more contentious, more mobile, more questioning, more alive to the fragility of things. Out of the wreckage of one world, they began, slowly and without knowing it, to construct another.

The bones are still there, under the cities of Europe and Asia, in the mass graves that archaeologists continue to find. They are a reminder that history is not only made by the living.
