The Daily Brief

Smoke and Silver: The Opium Wars and the Unmaking of Imperial China

---

Every empire believes, at its height, that its arrangements are eternal. The Qing court in Beijing had reason to feel confident. It presided over the most populous nation on earth, commanded tribute from vassal states across Central and Southeast Asia, and regarded European traders with a mixture of curiosity and mild contempt — useful barbarians, manageable at the margins. Then came the ships, the drug, and the cannons. And nothing in East Asia was ever quite the same again.

---

The World Before

To understand what the Opium Wars destroyed, you have to understand what existed before them — and it was something genuinely remarkable.

By the early nineteenth century, the Qing dynasty had ruled China for nearly two hundred years. At its territorial peak under the Qianlong Emperor, who reigned from 1735 to 1796, the empire stretched from the South China Sea to the steppes of Central Asia, encompassing Tibet, Xinjiang, Mongolia, and Manchuria. It was, by almost any measure, the largest contiguous land empire in Chinese history, and it was administered by a sophisticated bureaucracy of scholar-officials who had spent decades mastering the Confucian classics and passing grueling imperial examinations.

China's relationship with the outside world was carefully managed. Foreign trade was permitted, but only through designated channels. European merchants — the British East India Company most prominently — were confined to the port city of Canton (now Guangzhou) and required to conduct all business through a licensed guild of Chinese merchants called the Cohong. They lived in a narrow strip of factories along the Pearl River waterfront, forbidden from learning Chinese, forbidden from venturing into the city, forbidden from petitioning the emperor directly. The emperor, after all, had no need of foreign goods. When the British sent a grand diplomatic mission in 1793, led by Lord Macartney and bearing elaborate gifts of British manufacture — clocks, telescopes, artillery — the Qianlong Emperor famously replied that China "possesses all things in prolific abundance" and had no need of outside products.

This was not mere arrogance. It was, for much of Chinese history, an accurate description. China was a net exporter of silk, porcelain, and tea. European merchants paid for these goods largely in silver, which drained steadily westward into Chinese coffers. The trade imbalance was enormous, and it frustrated British merchants and the Crown's treasury alike. The question that preoccupied the East India Company was blunt and mercantile: what could they sell to China in sufficient quantities to reverse the flow of silver?

The answer they settled on was monstrous.

---

The Drug That Moved an Empire

Opium had existed in China for centuries, used medicinally in small quantities. But the version of the trade that the British East India Company engineered in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries was something different in kind — an industrial-scale narcotics operation, cultivated in British-controlled Bengal and shipped across the Bay of Bengal to Canton in purpose-built clipper ships fast enough to outrun most official interference.

The numbers are staggering in retrospect. In 1729, perhaps 200 chests of opium entered China annually. By 1838, the number had climbed to roughly 40,000 chests. Addiction spread from the coastal ports inland, touching soldiers, officials, merchants, and peasants alike. Silver began flowing out of China to pay for the drug, reversing the centuries-long current. The Qing treasury felt the drain. Local governments struggled to collect taxes as the silver supply tightened. The social fabric in affected regions began to fray visibly.

The Daoguang Emperor, who had ascended the throne in 1820, was not oblivious. He commissioned a series of reports and debated the matter extensively at court. Some officials argued for legalization and taxation, reasoning that prohibition had clearly failed. Others argued for intensified suppression. The emperor chose suppression, and in 1838 he appointed a man named Lin Zexu as Imperial Commissioner to Canton with instructions to end the trade by whatever means necessary.

Lin Zexu was, by any reasonable assessment, one of the more admirable figures in this story. He was incorruptible, energetic, and possessed of genuine moral clarity about what the opium trade was doing to his countrymen. He wrote a remarkable letter to Queen Victoria — it is uncertain whether she ever read it — appealing to her conscience and pointing out that Britain itself banned the domestic sale of opium. He argued, with considerable logic, that what was forbidden in England out of concern for English people ought not to be imposed on Chinese people for profit. The letter received no reply.

In the spring of 1839, Lin Zexu did something that made abstract diplomatic tensions suddenly very concrete. He surrounded the Canton factories, cut off food and water to the foreign merchants within, and demanded the surrender of all opium stocks. After weeks of standoff, the British superintendent of trade, Charles Elliot, agreed. More than 20,000 chests of opium — roughly 1,400 tons — were surrendered and destroyed. Lin Zexu's men mixed the drug with lime and salt and flushed it into the sea over the course of twenty-three days, reportedly offering ritual prayers to the ocean spirits in apology for the contamination.

In London, the destruction of British commercial property — whatever that property happened to be — was treated as an outrage. The debate in Parliament was not without dissent. William Gladstone, then a young MP, rose to condemn the coming war as unjust, calling it "a war more unjust in its origin, a war more calculated in its progress to cover this country with permanent disgrace." He was outvoted. The expeditionary force sailed.

---

Two Wars, One Long Humiliation

The First Opium War (1839–1842) was, militarily, a mismatch so complete that it barely deserves to be called a war in the conventional sense. British warships — steam-powered, iron-hulled, armed with modern artillery — moved along the Chinese coast and up the Yangtze River with near impunity. The Qing military, still organized around cavalry and musketry suited to the steppe warfare of two centuries earlier, could not effectively resist. Forts fell. Cities were threatened. The Daoguang Emperor, confronted with the reality of defeat, sued for peace.

The Treaty of Nanking (1842) was the first of what Chinese historians would come to call the "unequal treaties," a phrase that carries enormous weight in Chinese historical memory. China ceded Hong Kong island to Britain in perpetuity, opened five treaty ports to British residence and trade (Canton, Shanghai, Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Xiamen), paid an indemnity of 21 million silver dollars, and abolished the Cohong system that had previously managed foreign commerce. Most significantly, it established the principle of extraterritoriality — British subjects in China would be tried under British law, not Chinese law, in British courts. China's legal sovereignty over its own territory had been breached.

The Second Opium War (1856–1860), which pulled France into alliance with Britain, was triggered by a dispute over a ship called the Arrow and escalated with shocking speed into something that felt, to Chinese observers, like civilizational assault. British and French forces captured Canton, marched north, and in October 1860 sacked and burned the Yuanmingyuan — the Old Summer Palace outside Beijing — a complex of gardens, pavilions, and art collections that had taken a century to build and that the Qianlong Emperor had regarded as a jewel of his reign. The burning lasted two days. The commander who ordered it, Lord Elgin, reportedly justified it as a punishment directed at the emperor rather than the Chinese people. The distinction was lost on both.

The resulting Convention of Peking (1860) extended the concessions further. Kowloon was added to British Hong Kong. More ports were opened. The opium trade was legalized. Christian missionaries were granted the right to travel and own property throughout China. And Russia, which had helpfully offered to mediate, collected a vast swathe of territory north of the Amur River as its reward — land that China had administered for centuries.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Historians have argued at length about how to frame these events, and the debate is genuinely unresolved in interesting ways.

The older Western historiography tended to treat the Opium Wars as an unfortunate but perhaps inevitable collision between a modernizing West and a stagnant, insular Chinese empire — implicitly suggesting that China's "opening" was, in the long run, beneficial. This view has been largely abandoned by serious scholars, not least because it required ignoring the extraordinary human cost of the wars, the addiction epidemic, and the century of instability that followed.

More recent scholarship has complicated the picture in a different direction. Historians like James Polachek and others have examined the intense factional debates within the Qing court, recovering a Chinese political history that is far more dynamic and contested than the old narrative of imperial rigidity allowed. The question of whether China could have modernized on its own terms, without the violent disruption of Western imperialism, remains genuinely open — and genuinely important.

There is also the question of the opium trade's long-term economic effects. Some economic historians have argued that the silver drain caused by opium imports contributed directly to the fiscal crises that made the Qing state increasingly unable to manage internal rebellions — most devastatingly the Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864), which may have killed twenty to thirty million people and which unfolded in the shadow of the Opium Wars' aftermath. The causal chain from British drug policy to one of history's deadliest civil wars is not simple, but it is not imaginary either.

---

What Rose From the Ruins

The immediate aftermath of the Opium Wars was a China in crisis. The Qing state, its military humiliated and its finances strained, faced a series of internal rebellions — the Taiping, the Nian, the Muslim rebellions in Yunnan and the northwest — that collectively threatened to dissolve the dynasty entirely. That the Qing survived into the twentieth century is, in retrospect, somewhat surprising.

What survived alongside it was a new consciousness. Chinese reformers of the late nineteenth century — men like Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao — began the painful work of asking why China had lost and what needed to change. The "Self-Strengthening Movement" of the 1860s through 1890s attempted to adopt Western military technology while preserving Chinese cultural and political institutions — a formula that failed its definitive test in the Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895, when China was defeated not by a European power but by a neighbor that had embraced wholesale modernization after the Meiji Restoration.

The humiliation compounded. The Boxer Uprising of 1900, a millenarian anti-foreign movement, ended with a joint expeditionary force of eight nations marching into Beijing, looting the capital, and imposing yet another punishing indemnity. The Qing dynasty collapsed in 1912. The Republic that followed was weak, fractured, and prey to warlordism. Japan, which had learned the lessons of Western imperialism with disturbing efficiency, invaded Manchuria in 1931 and launched full-scale war against China in 1937. The century between the First Opium War and the founding of the People's Republic in 1949 is referred to in Chinese historiography as the "century of humiliation" — and the phrase is not mere rhetoric. It describes a specific, lived experience of sovereignty violated, territory lost, and national dignity systematically stripped away.

The People's Republic, when it came, understood itself explicitly as the answer to that century. The narrative of humiliation and restoration is not peripheral to Chinese Communist Party legitimacy — it is central to it, invoked regularly and with evident sincerity by leaders from Mao Zedong to Xi Jinping.

---

Why It Matters

The Opium Wars are not merely a chapter in the history of Chinese suffering. They are a case study in something that recurs across modern history with unsettling regularity: the willingness of powerful states to dress commercial interest in the language of principle, and to use overwhelming military force to pry open markets that would otherwise remain closed.

The British case was unusually brazen — the commodity being traded was an addictive drug, and the government knew it — but the underlying logic was not unique to Britain or to that era. The idea that free trade is a universal good, and that any state refusing to participate on the dominant power's terms is behaving unreasonably, has shown remarkable staying power as a justification for coercion.

The Opium Wars also illuminate the long political half-life of historical grievance. When China's leaders speak today of "core interests," of sovereignty as non-negotiable, of foreign interference as an existential threat, they are drawing on a well of historical memory that is both genuine and carefully maintained. Whether one agrees with specific Chinese foreign policy positions or not, understanding where that posture comes from — understanding that it is not paranoia but the accumulated lesson of a century — is indispensable to understanding the world we actually live in.

Finally, there is something worth sitting with in the figure of Lin Zexu, writing his unanswered letter to a queen, making a moral argument that everyone around him acknowledged was correct and that no one in power acted upon. The argument was right. The power was elsewhere. That gap — between the persuasiveness of a moral case and the indifference of those with the force to override it — is one of the oldest and most persistent problems in international life. The opium still moves. The ships are just different now.
