The Last Great Land Empire: How the Qing Dynasty Remade Central Asia

The Daily Brief — Early Modern History

---

The Hawk's Eye View

Imagine standing on the high steppe somewhere west of the Tianshan mountains in the summer of 1757. The grass rolls away in every direction, broken only by the occasional felt tent and the smoke of cooking fires. For centuries, this land has belonged to people who measure wealth in horses and sheep, who reckon distance in days of riding, and who have never paid taxes to any settled emperor. The nearest city of consequence is weeks away. The sky here is enormous, and the silence is the kind that makes empires seem like rumors.

Then, from the east, comes the army.

It arrives not as a ragged frontier force but as a disciplined, logistically sophisticated war machine — Manchu bannermen, Mongol cavalry auxiliaries, Han Chinese infantry, all supplied by a chain of granaries and relay stations stretching back thousands of miles to Beijing. They are fighting under the dragon banner of the Qing dynasty, and they have come to do something no Chinese emperor had ever managed to do before: conquer the western steppe for good.

What followed was one of the most consequential — and least discussed — imperial expansions of the early modern world.

---

The World Before

To understand what the Qing accomplished in Central Asia, you first have to understand what Central Asia was before the Qing arrived.

For most of recorded history, the great grasslands stretching from Mongolia westward through what is today Xinjiang, Kazakhstan, and beyond had been the domain of nomadic confederacies. These were not primitive societies. They were highly mobile, politically sophisticated, and militarily formidable. For centuries they had extracted tribute from settled civilizations rather than the other way around. The Xiongnu had extorted the Han dynasty. The Mongols had conquered half the known world. Even the Ming dynasty, one of the wealthiest states in human history, had largely given up trying to pacify the steppe and built walls instead.

By the early eighteenth century, the dominant power on the western steppe was the Dzungar Khanate — a confederation of Oirat Mongol tribes whose territory sprawled across a vast arc from the Altai mountains to Tibet. The Dzungars were not a spent force or a relic. Under leaders like Galdan in the late seventeenth century and Galdan Tseren in the early eighteenth, they had built a state capable of fielding tens of thousands of cavalry, maintaining diplomatic relations with the Russian Empire, and threatening Qing authority over Outer Mongolia itself. They were, in short, the last great nomadic empire.

The Qing, meanwhile, were themselves relative newcomers to imperial power. The Manchu people who founded the dynasty in 1644 had originally been semi-nomadic themselves, from the forests and river valleys of northeastern Manchuria. This gave the Qing a cultural and military advantage that the Ming had never possessed: they understood steppe warfare from the inside. They spoke Mongolian as a court language. They forged marriage alliances with Mongol princes. They adopted the Tibetan Buddhist faith as a tool of soft power across Inner Asia. In many ways, the Qing were not a Chinese dynasty that happened to rule the steppe — they were an Inner Asian empire that happened to also rule China.

This distinction matters enormously for what came next.

---

The Long War

The conflict between the Qing and the Dzungars was not a single campaign but a multigenerational struggle that stretched across nearly a century, from the 1680s to its violent conclusion in the late 1750s. It had the character of a cold war punctuated by hot ones — decades of diplomacy, espionage, and proxy fighting interrupted by major military campaigns.

The Kangxi Emperor, who ruled from 1661 to 1722, fought Galdan directly in a series of campaigns on the Mongolian steppe, defeating him in 1696 at the Battle of Jao Modo and driving him to suicide the following year. But the Dzungar state survived. It retreated westward, reconsolidated, and continued to press against Qing influence in Tibet and among the eastern Mongols. The Yongzheng Emperor tried and largely failed to deliver a decisive blow in the 1720s and 1730s. The steppe seemed to swallow armies.

It was the Qianlong Emperor — who ascended the throne in 1735 and would reign for sixty years — who finally resolved the question, with a ruthlessness that still unsettles historians.

By the 1750s, the Dzungar Khanate was weakening from within. A smallpox epidemic had devastated its population. Succession disputes had fractured its leadership, with rival princes seeking Qing support against each other. Qianlong, a confident and ambitious ruler who saw himself as a universal emperor in the Inner Asian tradition, recognized the opening. In 1755, he sent two armies westward — one by the northern route through Mongolia, one by the southern route through the Tarim Basin — and they swept through Dzungar territory with startling speed.

The initial campaign was almost shockingly easy. The Qing forces captured the Dzungar capital of Ghulja and installed a client ruler. It seemed the war was over.

It was not. Within a year, an anti-Qing rebellion had broken out, led by a Dzungar prince named Amursana, who had initially cooperated with the Qing but now demanded to be recognized as the supreme khan of all the Oirats. Qianlong refused. Amursana rebelled. The Qing armies returned, and this time the emperor gave orders that left no ambiguity about his intentions.

What followed in 1757 and 1758 was a campaign of annihilation. Qing forces killed Dzungar fighters wherever they found them. They drove populations from their pastures. Smallpox, famine, and flight completed what the armies began. The Dzungar people — who may have numbered anywhere from 500,000 to over a million before the conflict — were reduced by death, dispersal, and enslavement to a tiny remnant. Amursana fled north into Russian territory, where he died of smallpox in 1757.

The Dzungar Khanate ceased to exist. Not as a political entity forced into submission, but as a people nearly erased from the face of the earth.

---

Into the Tarim Basin

With the Dzungars destroyed, the Qing turned to the oasis cities of the Tarim Basin — the string of settlements at the foot of the Tianshan and Kunlun mountains that had once formed the Silk Road: Kashgar, Yarkand, Khotan, Aksu. This region was inhabited largely by Turkic-speaking Muslim peoples, the ancestors of today's Uyghurs, who had been under Dzungar domination and before that under various Chagatai successor states.

The Qing conquest of the Tarim Basin in 1759 completed the annexation of what they would call Xinjiang — the "New Frontier" or "New Territory," a name that carries its own political freight down to the present day. The conquest was accomplished with a combination of military force and political accommodation. The Qing established a garrison system, staffing key cities with Manchu and Mongol bannermen while allowing local Muslim elites — the begs — to continue administering their communities under Qing supervision.

This was empire by layering rather than by replacement. The Qing did not try to make the Uyghurs into Manchus or Chinese. They governed through a system of indirect rule, collecting taxes and maintaining order while leaving local religious and cultural life largely intact — at least in the early decades. The great mosques continued to stand. The begs continued to adjudicate local disputes. The Qing emperor presented himself not just as the Son of Heaven but as the patron of Tibetan Buddhism and, implicitly, as a sovereign whose power encompassed all the peoples of Inner Asia regardless of their faith.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Here is where historians begin to argue, sometimes fiercely.

The question of how to characterize the Qing expansion into Central Asia has become one of the most contested issues in the study of Chinese and Inner Asian history. For much of the twentieth century, Chinese historiography framed the Qing campaigns as the "reunification" of territories that had always been part of China — a narrative that carries obvious political implications for the status of Xinjiang today. On this reading, the Qing were not conquerors but restorers, bringing wayward lands back into a civilization that had always encompassed them.

A generation of scholars associated with what became known as the "New Qing History" — historians like Pamela Kyle Crossley, Mark Elliott, and Peter Perdue — pushed back hard against this framing. Their argument, rooted in close reading of Manchu-language sources that earlier scholars had largely ignored, is that the Qing were not a Chinese dynasty in any straightforward sense but a multiethnic Inner Asian empire. The Qianlong Emperor did not conquer Central Asia as a Chinese emperor extending Chinese civilization westward. He conquered it as a Manchu ruler extending a specifically Manchu imperial project — one that drew on steppe traditions of universal sovereignty as much as on Confucian statecraft.

This matters because it changes the moral and political valence of the expansion. If the Qing conquest of Xinjiang was an Inner Asian empire absorbing neighboring Inner Asian peoples, it looks rather different than if it was "China" incorporating "Chinese territory." The former is empire in the conventional sense; the latter is something else, and exactly what it is remains genuinely contested.

There is also a darker debate: whether the destruction of the Dzungars constitutes, by modern definitions, genocide. Peter Perdue, in his landmark study China Marches West, treats the demographic catastrophe unflinchingly and notes that Qianlong's orders explicitly called for the killing of men and the enslavement of women and children. Others argue that disease, rather than deliberate slaughter, accounts for most of the mortality. The distinction matters legally and morally, but the scale of the death is not seriously disputed. A civilization was extinguished.

---

The Aftermath

The incorporation of Xinjiang transformed the Qing Empire into the largest territorial state in Chinese history — and one of the largest in the world. The new borders pushed Qing sovereignty to the edge of the Pamirs, bringing the empire into direct contact with the Russian Empire expanding from the north and west. The two empires would spend the next century negotiating the boundaries of their respective influence across Central Asia in a slow-motion diplomatic contest that anticipated the later "Great Game" between Russia and Britain.

Within Xinjiang itself, the Qing imported thousands of Han Chinese settlers to repopulate the land emptied by the destruction of the Dzungars. They brought in Hui Muslims from the interior. They resettled Mongol groups. The demographic mosaic of modern Xinjiang — complex, layered, and politically explosive — was in many ways created by Qing policy in the late eighteenth century.

The Qing also faced persistent resistance. A series of Muslim uprisings in the nineteenth century, led by figures drawing on the authority of the Naqshbandi Sufi orders, repeatedly challenged Qing control. The great rebellion of 1864–1877, which coincided with the Dungan Revolt in the interior, temporarily expelled Qing forces from most of the region. The eventual reconquest under Zuo Zongtang led to the formal incorporation of Xinjiang as a province of the Chinese state in 1884 — a transformation from military frontier to administrative province that reflected a new, more explicitly "Chinese" conception of sovereignty.

When the Qing dynasty fell in 1912, it left the Republic of China an enormous territorial inheritance — one that the People's Republic of China would later claim in full, including Xinjiang, Tibet, and Inner Mongolia. The wars of the 1750s thus cast a very long shadow.

---

Why It Matters

The Qing expansion into Central Asia is a story about the end of an era and the beginning of another. It marks the final chapter of nomadic power on the Eurasian steppe — the closing of a world that had existed since the first horse was tamed on the grasslands north of the Black Sea. After the Dzungars, no steppe confederation would ever again threaten a settled empire. The age of the nomad was over, not because the steppe peoples were somehow primitive or doomed, but because gunpowder, logistics, and the bureaucratic state had tilted the balance of power irrevocably toward sedentary civilizations.

But the story is also about something more immediate and urgent: the way that imperial histories become the foundations — and the justifications — of modern political claims. The borders of contemporary China, the status of Xinjiang, the rights and identity of the Uyghur people — all of these live inside the history of what happened on the steppe in the 1750s. You cannot understand the present crisis without understanding the Qianlong Emperor's campaigns, the destruction of the Dzungars, and the long, contested process by which a military frontier became a province became a "core interest."

History does not determine the present, but it furnishes the arguments people use to fight about it. Knowing the history clearly — in all its complexity, violence, and ambiguity — is the beginning of honest engagement with the world as it actually is.
