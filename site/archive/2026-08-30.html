The Daily Brief

The Hundred Years' War and the Birth of Nations

---

A King Dies Without Sons

In the winter of 1328, the last Capetian king of France, Charles IV, drew his final breath in the royal palace, leaving behind a queen who was pregnant but not yet delivered. The court held its breath. If the child was a boy, the crisis was averted. If a girl — or worse, a stillbirth — the most powerful throne in Christendom would fall into a succession dispute that had no clean answer.

The queen delivered a daughter.

What followed was not simply a squabble over a crown. It was the slow ignition of a conflict that would burn for more than a century, reshape two kingdoms, birth the idea of the nation-state, produce one of history's most improbable military figures, and leave scars on the European landscape — physical, cultural, and psychological — that are still faintly visible today. We call it the Hundred Years' War, though almost no one alive during it would have recognized the name. To them, it was simply the war. The long war. The war that never seemed to end.

---

The World Before

To understand what was at stake, you have to first disabuse yourself of the notion that medieval France and England were anything like the nations we know today. In 1300, the map of Western Europe was a patchwork of overlapping loyalties, feudal obligations, and dynastic claims that made modern borders look almost naively clean.

The English king held vast territories in France — Gascony in the southwest, remnants of the old Angevin empire that had once stretched from the Scottish border to the Pyrenees. He held these lands not as a sovereign, but as a vassal of the French crown. This meant the King of England, one of the most powerful rulers in Europe, was technically required to kneel before the King of France and swear homage. You can imagine how well that sat with successive English monarchs.

Meanwhile, the French crown had been steadily consolidating power, absorbing duchies and counties, turning regional lords into royal subjects. The Capetian dynasty — the same family that had held the French throne in an unbroken line since 987 — had been remarkably successful at this centralizing project. But it had also created resentments, and it had produced, in its final generation, three kings in a row who died without male heirs.

Into this volatile situation stepped two claimants: Philip of Valois, a French nobleman and cousin of the dead king, and Edward III of England, whose claim came through his mother, Isabella — the daughter of Philip IV of France. Edward was, by bloodline, a grandson of a French king. Philip was merely a nephew. By the strictest reading of hereditary right, Edward had the stronger claim.

But France had other ideas. French lawyers and nobles invoked an ancient Frankish legal code called Salic law, which barred inheritance through the female line. Whether Salic law actually applied to the French throne was, at the time, genuinely debatable — it had never been tested in quite this way. But Philip had the advantage of being on the ground, being French, and being male. He was crowned Philip VI, and Edward, for the moment, swallowed his objection.

---

The Long War Begins

The peace was never comfortable. Tensions over Gascony, over English wool trade with Flemish cities that France claimed as vassals, over Edward's harboring of French rebels — all of it accumulated like kindling. In 1337, Philip declared Edward's French territories forfeit. Edward responded by formally claiming the French crown itself. The war had begun, though almost no one understood yet what kind of war it would be.

The first decades belonged to England with shocking completeness. At Crécy in 1346, Edward III's army — outnumbered, tired from a long march, and fighting on foreign soil — demolished a French force of knights with the Welsh longbow, a weapon that could drive an arrow through armor at two hundred yards. The flower of French chivalry died in the mud of a Picardy hillside. Ten years later, Edward's son — the Black Prince, Edward of Woodstock — repeated the performance at Poitiers, and this time captured the French king himself, John II, who was ransomed for an almost incomprehensible sum.

For France, these were years of catastrophe piled upon catastrophe. The Black Death had arrived in 1347, killing perhaps a third of the population. Bands of unemployed soldiers called routiers or free companies roamed the countryside when the armies were not actively fighting, pillaging villages and extorting towns. The peasantry erupted in a savage rebellion called the Jacquerie in 1358. The kingdom seemed to be dissolving from within.

And yet it did not dissolve. This is, in retrospect, one of the most remarkable things about the Hundred Years' War — the way France, repeatedly broken open, repeatedly found some inner coherence that pulled it back together. Part of the credit belongs to Charles V, the scholarly king who came to power in 1364 and spent his reign rebuilding the French military, reforming taxation, and — crucially — avoiding the pitched battles that the English longbow made so lethal. His general, Bertrand du Guesclin, fought a war of harassment and attrition, retaking towns one by one. By 1380, the English held only Calais and a strip of coastline. The war seemed nearly over.

---

The Darkness Before the Dawn

Then everything fell apart again. Henry V of England, young, ferociously capable, and possessed of a political genius that matched his military talent, reopened the conflict in 1415. At Agincourt — another arrow-storm catastrophe for French knights — he humiliated French arms before the whole of Europe. By 1420, the Treaty of Troyes had declared Henry heir to the French throne, married him to a French princess, and disinherited the French Dauphin, Charles, who was widely rumored (almost certainly falsely) to be illegitimate.

Henry V died of dysentery in 1422, before he could claim his prize. The French king died weeks later. The infant Henry VI was proclaimed king of both realms. The Dauphin refused to accept it.

France was now split: the north occupied by the English and their Burgundian allies, the south loyal to the Dauphin's fragile court at Bourges. The war ground on, and the English were winning. By 1428, they were besieging Orléans, the gateway to the south. If Orléans fell, Charles's cause was probably finished.

It was at this precise moment — the lowest point of French fortunes, the moment when the kingdom seemed genuinely about to be extinguished — that a teenage girl from a village on the eastern frontier presented herself at the Dauphin's court and announced that God had sent her to save France.

Joan of Arc is one of those historical figures who defeats easy explanation. She was perhaps seventeen years old, illiterate, from a farming family in Domrémy. She claimed to hear the voices of saints — Catherine, Margaret, Michael — who commanded her to drive out the English and see the Dauphin crowned at Reims, the traditional site of French coronations. Charles, desperate and perhaps intrigued, gave her an army.

What happened next astonished everyone. Joan lifted the siege of Orléans in nine days. She led the French army in a lightning campaign up the Loire Valley, winning battle after battle. In July 1429, Charles was crowned at Reims, the crown of France placed on his head in the cathedral where French kings had been anointed for centuries. The symbolic power was enormous. Whatever the lawyers said about Salic law and treaty rights, here was the anointed King of France, crowned in the sacred city, by the grace of God and a peasant girl who heard voices.

Joan was captured by the Burgundians in 1430, sold to the English, tried for heresy by a church court, and burned at the stake in Rouen in 1431. She was nineteen years old. The English thought they had destroyed her. They had, in fact, made her immortal.

---

The Scholarly Debate

Historians have long wrestled with what, exactly, the Hundred Years' War was about — and what it created. For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, scholars told it as a story of emerging nationalism: the war forged the French and English nations, gave both peoples a sense of distinct identity, and in doing so, laid the groundwork for the modern world.

More recent scholarship has complicated this picture considerably. Historians like John Watts and David Bell have argued that "national identity" in the modern sense was still a distant aspiration in 1400, and that we risk projecting our own categories backward. What people felt was loyalty to a king, to a region, to a community — not to an abstract nation. The war may have intensified certain cultural distinctions between English and French, but it did not create nationalism so much as create the conditions in which nationalism would eventually become thinkable.

Joan herself remains a mystery in several respects. The voices she heard, the nature of her faith, the question of whether her trial was a genuine theological proceeding or a purely political execution — these have been debated for six centuries. Her rehabilitation trial of 1456, which cleared her name posthumously, and her eventual canonization in 1920, have layered later meanings onto a figure who was already complex. What we can say with confidence is that she mattered enormously in her own time, and that the French army that fought on after her death fought differently than it had before her.

---

The Aftermath

The war ended not with a dramatic battle but with a gradual English collapse. The Burgundians switched sides. English territories in France fell one by one. In 1453, the English were defeated at Castillon — the last major battle — and expelled from Bordeaux, their last significant holding in the south. Only Calais remained, and it would not fall until 1558. There was no peace treaty. The war simply stopped.

What rose from the wreckage was remarkable. France emerged with a monarchy far more powerful than the one that had entered the war. The long emergency had given French kings the justification to levy permanent taxes, maintain standing armies, and centralize authority in ways that would have been politically impossible in 1337. The feudal nobility, bled white by a century of war, was weaker. The crown was stronger. The administrative machinery of the French state — the machinery that would eventually become absolute monarchy, and then, after revolution, the modern French republic — was built in the fires of the Hundred Years' War.

England's trajectory was different but equally consequential. The loss of France was a trauma that echoed for generations, but it also forced England inward. With no continental empire to defend, England turned toward its own internal politics — with results that included the Wars of the Roses, and eventually, the Reformation. The English language itself was changed: the long war against France made it politically uncomfortable to speak French, and the vernacular English that Chaucer was already writing gained new prestige and legitimacy.

And Joan? Twenty-five years after her execution, a French king who owed his crown in large part to her sacrifice ordered a new trial. The verdict of 1431 was overturned. She was not a heretic. She was a martyr. France has never entirely let her go.

---

Why It Matters

The Hundred Years' War is easy to file away as a medieval curiosity — a dynastic squabble dressed up in armor and arrow-flights. But its deeper currents run directly into the present.

The war demonstrated, with terrible clarity, that the medieval idea of overlapping loyalties and shared sovereignty could not survive indefinitely. Sooner or later, a king had to choose: was he king of England, or a vassal of France? The answer shaped everything. The nation-state — the idea that a people and a territory and a government ought to align, that sovereignty must be singular and bounded — did not spring fully formed from the Hundred Years' War. But the war was one of the crucibles in which that idea was tested, refined, and eventually made to seem natural and inevitable.

Joan of Arc speaks to something else entirely: the enduring human capacity to be moved by a figure who seems to arrive from outside the normal order of things. Every age produces its Joans — people who cut through the accumulated cynicism and bureaucratic logic of institutions to insist that something impossible is not only possible but required. History does not always vindicate them. In her case, eventually, it did.

And the war's long duration — one hundred and sixteen years, outlasting every individual who started it — is itself a lesson in the way conflicts develop their own momentum, their own logic, their own reasons for continuing long after the original cause has been forgotten. The heirs of Edward III did not really want the French crown; they wanted leverage, revenue, glory. The heirs of Philip VI did not really fear English rule; they feared disorder, taxation, and the loss of what they had. Wars, the Hundred Years' War reminds us, are rarely about what they say they are about.
